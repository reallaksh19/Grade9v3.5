## II. Proposed teaching architecture: six roots, distinct branches

Six useful *conceptual root categories* emerge; each may need more than one teachable microtopic, and no predetermined one-page-per-root quota applies.

1. **Signed expression structure:** subtracting polynomials, preserving monomial identity and coefficient/sign distribution.
2. **Affine modelling:** \(y=mx+b\), measurement units, fixed/variable decomposition, relative cost, and table-to-rule inference. These branches are not automatically interchangeable.
3. **Constraint and parameter interpretation:** a point on a line, coupled equations and when evidence suffices for a unique answer.
4. **Time-dependent and ratio-dependent relationships:** age invariance and income/expenditure/savings; these are separate constructions even though both use equations.
5. **Algebraic identity geometry:** why \((a+b)^2\) includes two \(ab\) cross terms; the visual decomposition should be mathematically bound to area components.
6. **Polynomial product/zero structure:** factoring polynomial volume forms and identifying distinct linear factors from polynomial roots; these are different inferences within a shared multiplication/factorization foundation.

## III. Reference Core1A construction A — fixed and variable contributions

**Inferential jump:** An affine rule contains an additive invariant \(b\) and a repeated unit contribution \(mx\); they are different quantities, with different units.

- Entry: multiplying a quantity by a rate, reading ordered pairs, addition with compatible units, solving a linear equation.
- Declare units: if \(n\) is copies, \(v\) has currency/copy and \(F\) currency, then \(C(n)=F+vn\) has currency. This explicit dimensional bridge prevents \(Fn+v\).
- Authored worked example: a service has a fixed cost of 150 currency units and costs 12 units per printed copy. \(C(n)=150+12n\). Then \(C(5)=210\). The difference \(C(6)-C(5)=12\) is the per-unit slope, while \(C(0)=150\) is the formal intercept; if zero copies cannot be ordered, mark that intercept as model extrapolation rather than an actual purchasable service.
- Wrong path: \(C(n)=150n+12\) swaps the fixed and variable components. Diagnostic: compare \(C(1)\) with \(C(2)\) and ask *which component repeats on every new item*.
- Representation: a two-column table (copies, total cost) plus a graph showing a 150 intercept and repeated rise of 12 for each unit; every mark must be tied to the algebra.
- Applicability boundary: a constant per-copy price and fixed fee; tiered pricing or a maximum batch discount does not follow this same affine rule.
- Independent exit: a delivery costs 80 currency units fixed plus 7 per kilogram. Construct \(C(m)\) and calculate \(C(9)\). Model closure: \(80+7m;\ C(9)=143\), and \(C(10)-C(9)=7\).
- Genuine transfer candidate: infer \(F,v\) from two observations **only after** deciding the model is affine (e.g. \(C(2)=74\) and \(C(5)=110\) imply \(v=12,F=50\)). This extra inference is not automatically a Core2B task: prove it changes a protected model decision relative to prior exposure.

## IV. Reference construction B — identifying what given points establish

**Inferential jump:** Substitution yields an *equation about unknown coefficients*, not necessarily a unique answer. Uniqueness depends on the number and independence of available constraints.

- Entry: a point \((x_0,y_0)\) lies on \(ax+by=c\) precisely when \(ax_0+by_0=c\).
- Authored worked anchor: \((2,3)\) lies on \(ax+by=12\). Therefore \(2a+3b=12\). By itself this gives infinitely many pairs \((a,b)\); examples \((a,b)=(3,2)\) and \((0,4)\) both work. Adding a **separate** condition \(a-b=1\), obtain \(a=3,b=2\) and verify both original constraints.
- Wrong path: set \(a=2,b=3\) merely by matching the coordinates; or claim a unique pair from one equation. Diagnostic: require **two distinct parameter pairs** satisfying the single-point condition before allowing a unique-solution claim.
- Representation: a constraint table with rows “evidence”, “equation inferred”, “independent information”, and “permitted conclusion”. If a geometric diagram is used, show why the same point can belong to several different lines.
- Boundary: two equations may still be dependent or inconsistent; “two equations” alone does not guarantee one solution.
- Independent exit: \((1,2)\) satisfies \(px+qy=11\) and a separate condition \(p-q=2\). Model: \(p+2q=11\); \(p=q+2\); \(q=3,p=5\). Verify \(5+6=11\) and \(5-3=2\).
- Teaching link caution: the first task may require merely substitution, the second parameter uniqueness. Do not conflate those source cruxes without verifying printed conditions.

## V. Reference construction C — age timelines vs budget ratios

**These belong to separate concept branches, despite sharing linear equations.**

**Age branch:** age differences are invariant under a shared time shift, but ratios generally are not.

- Authored worked anchor: A is 9 years older than B; in 3 years A's age will be twice B's. Let present B \(=b\), A \(=b+9\); after 3 years \(b+12=2(b+3)\), so \(b=6\), A=15. Reverse-check future 18 and 9.
- Wrong path: add 3 years to only one person's age, or double the present ratio as though it cannot change. Show both people on a two-row timeline.
- Independent exit: A is 7 years older than B; in 5 years A's age will be \(3/2\) of B's. Model: \(b+12=\tfrac32(b+5)\), \(b=9\), A=16; after 5 years 21 vs 14.
- Boundary: shared time flow and physically reasonable nonnegative ages; negative solutions flag an invalid model or impossible stated conditions.

**Budget ratio branch:** \(I:E=m:n\) means \(I=mk\) and \(E=nk\) for one positive scale \(k\), while savings \(S=I-E\).

- Authored worked anchor: income:expenditure \(=5:4\), savings 1,500. \(I=5k,E=4k\); \(k=1500\); hence \(I=7500,E=6000\).
- Wrong path: use \(I=5S\) and \(E=4S\) by default when the ratio difference is not 1. Diagnostic use ratio \(7:5\) so \(S=2k\).
- Independent exit: income:expenditure \(=7:5\), savings 2,000. Then \(2k=2000\), \(k=1000\), \(I=7000,E=5000\).
- Boundary: if spending exceeds income the assumed “positive savings” convention fails. Define whether a negative balance/debt is allowed before using the expression.

## VI. Reference construction D — table-to-law is inference, not proof

**Source-facing candidate:** organizer sample Q4. **Crux:** Identify a constant output difference per equal input increment, divide to obtain rate, then recover the intercept. A finite table supports but does not logically prove an unrestricted law.

- Authored worked anchor: given \((x,y)=(1,5),(3,11),(5,17)\) and **assume the model is affine**, slope \(m=(11-5)/(3-1)=3\). Intercept \(b=5-3(1)=2\). Thus \(y=3x+2\); all observed pairs check.
- Wrong path: take raw \(11-5=6\) as slope, ignoring input increase 2; or claim the data alone forces an affine rule for *every possible x*.
- Counterexample to inference certainty: \(g(x)=3x+2+(x-1)(x-3)(x-5)\) matches the same three observed points yet is not affine. Only an explicit model assumption or further evidence justifies the generalization.
- Representation: aligned step-table (\(\Delta x,\Delta y,\Delta y/\Delta x\)) linked to slope-triangle on the coordinate graph.
- Independent exit under stated affine assumption: points \((0,7),(2,15),(4,23)\). Infer \(m=4,b=7\), then predict \(y(6)=31\).
- Boundary: nonuniform x spacing needs rate per x-unit; unequal ratios indicate a nonaffine model or errors in data.

## VII. Reference construction E — algebraic expressions and areas

## VIII. Reference construction F — polynomial factors and constraints

**Factor-from-roots branch:** In polynomials over the reals, \(P(a)=0\iff(x-a)\) divides \(P(x)\). If \(a\ne b\) and both are roots, the distinct linear factors are coprime and \((x-a)(x-b)\) divides \(P(x)\).

- Authored example: \(P(x)=(x-2)(x-3)(x+1)=x^3-4x^2+x+6\). Check \(P(2)=P(3)=0\), then quotient by the two factors is \(x+1\).
- Wrong path: infer \((x-a)^2\) divides \(P\) merely because the *same* root condition is stated twice. Counterexample \(P=x-a\) itself.
- Exit: \(Q(x)=x^3-2x^2-5x+6\). Verify \(Q(1)=Q(3)=0\); factor \(Q=(x-1)(x-3)(x+2)\).
- Boundary: a root-based factor theorem concerns polynomial identity over a specified coefficient domain, not division by an arbitrary expression that can vanish on the working domain. A degree-one polynomial cannot have two distinct roots unless it is identically zero.

**Volume-factor branch:** A cubic physical-volume polynomial must factor into admissible positive linear dimensions **only where that interpretation is meaningful**.

- Authored anchor: \(V(x)=x^3+6x^2+11x+6=(x+1)(x+2)(x+3)\); for positive lengths require \(x>-1\). Check by expansion, then compare volume dimension \(L^3\) and three linear factors.
- Exit: factor \(x^3+9x^2+26x+24=(x+2)(x+3)(x+4)\) and state \(x>-2\) for positive edge lengths. Verify constant term \(2\cdot3\cdot4=24\).
- Boundary: polynomial factorization is algebraic, whereas a cuboid interpretation imposes positive lengths and compatible length units. Do not derive geometric authority from bare factorization alone.
