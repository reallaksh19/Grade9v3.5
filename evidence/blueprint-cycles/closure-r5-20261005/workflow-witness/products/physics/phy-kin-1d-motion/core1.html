One-dimensional motion: Orientation map

Path travelled is not start-to-finish change

Subject: PhysicsTopic: MotionConcept: Path travelled is not start-to-finish changeConcept difficulty: Medium concept

Hard transition

Distance counts the whole route travelled, while displacement records only the directed change from start to finish; a return to the start leaves zero displacement after a nonzero journey.

Before you go on

Route A leaves a marker and returns to it. Route B travels the same total amount but ends away from the marker. Without calculating a value, compare their distance and displacement.

Zero velocity at an instant does not mean zero acceleration

Subject: PhysicsTopic: MotionConcept: Zero velocity at an instant does not mean zero accelerationConcept difficulty: Medium concept

Hard transition

Velocity and acceleration answer different questions: velocity can be zero at one instant while a nonzero acceleration determines the change through that instant.

Before you go on

Two objects have zero velocity at one instant: one is at the top of a vertical flight, the other is resting on a shelf. What can you conclude about their accelerations from velocity alone?

Average speed and average velocity come from different totals

Subject: PhysicsTopic: MotionConcept: Average speed and average velocity come from different totalsConcept difficulty: Medium concept

Hard transition

Average speed divides total path length by total time, while average velocity divides signed displacement by that same time; neither is obtained by averaging listed speeds unless the weighting justifies it.

Governing relation

speed_avg = distance_total / Delta t

Average speed is total distance travelled divided by the positive elapsed time.

  • Delta t > 0.
  • distance_total >= 0.

v_avg = Delta x / Delta t

Average velocity is signed displacement divided by the same positive elapsed time.

  • Delta t > 0.
  • Delta x uses one declared axis/sign convention.

Before you go on

A trip covers 120 m of total path, finishes 40 m in the positive direction from its start, and lasts 20 s. Find the average speed and average velocity.

Derive the constant-acceleration equations from one velocity-time graph

Subject: PhysicsTopic: MotionConcept: Derive the constant-acceleration equations from one velocity-time graphConcept difficulty: Medium concept

Hard transition

One straight velocity-time segment carries all three Grade 9 kinematic equations: its slope gives v = u + at, its rectangle-plus-triangle area gives s = ut + 0.5at^2, and combining trapezoid area with the slope relation eliminates time to give v^2 = u^2 + 2as.

Governing relation

a_avg = Delta v / Delta t

Average acceleration over an interval is signed velocity change divided by the positive elapsed time.

  • Delta t > 0.
  • Velocity values use one declared one-dimensional sign convention.

s = u t + 0.5 (v-u) t = 0.5 (u+v) t

For constant acceleration, signed displacement over the interval is the signed geometric area under the straight velocity-time segment.

  • Acceleration is constant over the whole interval, so the velocity-time segment is straight.
  • u, v and s use one declared one-dimensional sign convention.
  • t >= 0.

v = u + a t

For constant acceleration over the interval, final velocity equals initial velocity plus acceleration times elapsed time.

  • Acceleration is constant over the whole interval.
  • u, v and a use one declared one-dimensional sign convention.
  • t >= 0.

s = u t + 0.5 a t^2

For constant acceleration, signed displacement equals the initial-velocity contribution plus the acceleration contribution.

  • Acceleration is constant over the whole interval.
  • s, u and a use one declared one-dimensional sign convention.
  • t >= 0.

v^2 = u^2 + 2 a s

For constant acceleration, squared velocities and signed displacement satisfy the no-time kinematic relation.

  • Acceleration is constant over the whole interval.
  • u, v, a and s use one declared one-dimensional sign convention.

Before you go on

A velocity-time graph is a straight segment from 5 m/s at t = 0 to 11 m/s at t = 3 s. Use the graph slope to identify a, use its rectangle-plus-triangle area to find displacement, and show how the same graph yields v = u + at, s = ut + 0.5at^2, and v^2 = u^2 + 2as.

Uniform circular motion keeps speed while velocity turns

Subject: PhysicsTopic: MotionConcept: Uniform circular motion keeps speed while velocity turnsConcept difficulty: Medium concept

Hard transition

An object moving around a circle at constant speed is still accelerating because velocity direction changes continuously; velocity is tangent, acceleration points inward, and the constant speed for one revolution is circumference divided by period: v = 2 pi r / T.

Governing relation

v = 2 pi r / T

For one complete uniform circular revolution, speed equals circumference divided by the positive period.

  • r > 0.
  • T > 0.
  • Motion is uniform and the path is circular over the measured revolution.

Before you go on

A runner moves uniformly around a circular track of radius 7 m and completes one revolution in 14 s. Find the speed, then state the qualitative directions of velocity and acceleration at any point.

Motion graphs turn coordinate change into rate

Subject: PhysicsTopic: MotionConcept: Motion graphs turn coordinate change into rateConcept difficulty: Medium concept

Hard transition

On a position-time graph, segment slope is average velocity over that interval; on a velocity-time graph, segment slope is average acceleration. Graph height and graph slope answer different physical questions.

Governing relation

v_avg = Delta x / Delta t

Average velocity is signed displacement divided by the same positive elapsed time.

  • Delta t > 0.
  • Delta x uses one declared axis/sign convention.

a_avg = Delta v / Delta t

Average acceleration over an interval is signed velocity change divided by the positive elapsed time.

  • Delta t > 0.
  • Velocity values use one declared one-dimensional sign convention.

Before you go on

On a velocity-time graph, velocity changes from +3 m/s at 2 s to +5 m/s at 4 s. Find the average acceleration over that interval and state what a horizontal velocity-time segment would mean.

Circular dynamics: centripetal is a net-force role, not a new force

Subject: PhysicsTopic: MotionConcept: Circular dynamics: centripetal is a net-force role, not a new forceConcept difficulty: Hard concept

Hard transition

The inward acceleration requirement v^2/r fixes only the net radial component m v^2/r; tension, friction, gravity, normal force, or combinations may supply that resultant, so 'centripetal force' must not be added as another interaction.

Governing relation

a_r = v^2 / r

For uniform circular motion of speed v on a path of positive radius r, the acceleration magnitude toward the centre is v^2/r.

  • r > 0.
  • The path is circular at the represented instant.
  • For the acceleration to be exactly radial with no tangential component, speed is constant at that instant/over the uniform model.

Sigma F_r = m v^2 / r

With inward chosen as positive radial direction, the signed sum of real radial force components equals m v^2/r for uniform circular motion.

  • Choose one body and declare inward as positive radial direction before resolving forces.
  • Only real interactions appear in an inertial-frame free-body diagram.
  • The represented motion has radial acceleration v^2/r.
  • Do not add an extra force named centripetal; Sigma F_r is the resultant radial role of the real forces.

T + m g = m v_top^2 / r

At the top of a vertical circle for a mass on a taut string, inward is downward so tension and weight both contribute positively to the radial resultant.

  • The body is at the top of the vertical circle.
  • The string is taut and massless in the bounded model.
  • Gravity acts downward and the radial positive direction is inward (downward at the top).

T - m g = m v_bottom^2 / r

At the bottom of a vertical circle for a mass on a taut string, inward is upward so tension contributes positively and weight negatively to the radial resultant.

  • The body is at the bottom of the vertical circle.
  • The string is taut and massless in the bounded model.
  • Gravity acts downward and the radial positive direction is inward (upward at the bottom).

v_min = sqrt(g r)

At the just-taut top boundary T=0, the vertical-circle top radial equation gives v_min=sqrt(gr).

  • The body is at the top of the vertical circle.
  • The string is at the just-taut boundary, so T = 0.
  • r > 0 and g > 0.
  • This is a boundary result for the declared string model, not a generic circular-motion formula.

Before you go on

A 2 kg mass moves at 4 m/s in a horizontal circle of radius 2 m on a string. Draw the inertial-frame horizontal FBD and find the required inward net force. Then state the immediate path if the string is cut.
