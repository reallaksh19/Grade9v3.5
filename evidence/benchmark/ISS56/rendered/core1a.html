Polynomial Interpolation, Degree Bounds, and Vanishing Cosets

Subject: MathematicsTopic: PolynomialsConcept: Polynomial Interpolation, Degree Bounds, and Vanishing CosetsConcept difficulty: Hard concept
1

Determine when finite data uniquely determine a polynomial and characterize all solutions when the degree bound is removed

Hard concept
Section 1 of 3Next →

Conditions for this construction

  • Evaluating polynomials at integer inputs.
  • Factor Theorem for finding linear factors (x - r).
Why this section matters for the question set

This unit builds toward

  • Q1 · D4

  1. Inspect finite data sample points and formulate a candidate interpolating polynomial.
    Why valid: Evaluation identifies a low-degree polynomial that matches the given sample points.
    Result: candidate polynomial
  2. Form the difference polynomial d(x) = p(x) - q(x) and count its distinct zeros.
    Why valid: Each agreement point x_i is a root of the difference polynomial d(x_i) = 0.
    Result: factored difference polynomial with known roots
  3. The step the hard question turns onApply the degree bound: if deg(d) < number of distinct roots, d(x) is identically zero.
    Why valid: A non-zero polynomial of degree n can have at most n roots by the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra.
    Result: uniqueness proof under degree bound
  4. Remove the degree bound and express the general solution as an affine coset modulo the vanishing product.
    Why valid: Any polynomial vanishing on the sample set is an arbitrary polynomial multiple of the vanishing product.
    Result: affine coset characterization

The key step

Agreement of polynomials at k inputs proves identity if and only if their degrees are strictly less than k; otherwise, solutions form an infinite coset parameterized by the product of linear factors.

Formula reference · meaning and conditions

Equations and validity

EquationWhat it tells youWhen you can use it

p(x) - q(x) = \prod_{i=1}^k (x - x_i) \cdot r(x)

If polynomials p and q agree at k distinct inputs, their difference is divisible by the degree-k vanishing product; if deg(p-q) < k, then p = q identically.

  • p, q are polynomials with real coefficients.
  • Points x_1, ..., x_k are pairwise distinct.

Worked explanation

Q1 · Owner-supplied question, verbatim

Exact data give $p(0)=1$, $p(1)=2$, $p(2)=5$ and $p(3)=10$. First assume $p$ is a real polynomial of degree at most three: determine it and justify uniqueness. Then remove the degree bound and characterise every real polynomial consistent with the same four data points. State precisely which inference depended on the bound.

Follow the operation, its justification and its result.

  1. Inspect the data points (0,1), (1,2), (2,5), (3,10) and observe the quadratic pattern p(k) = k^2 + 1.

    Why valid: Evaluation shows 0^2+1=1, 1^2+1=2, 2^2+1=5, 3^2+1=10, identifying a degree-2 candidate.

    Result: candidate polynomial x^2 + 1

  2. Form the difference polynomial d(x) = p(x) - (x^2 + 1) and count its zeros.

    Why valid: d(x) vanishes at all four given inputs: x = 0, 1, 2, 3.

    Result: d(x) has 4 distinct real zeros

  3. Apply the degree bound deg(p) <= 3 to establish uniqueness of p(x) = x^2 + 1.

    Why valid: Since deg(d) <= 3 and d has 4 roots, d(x) must be identically zero by the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra / Polynomial Identity Theorem.

    Result: p(x) = x^2 + 1 uniquely

  4. Remove the degree bound and characterize all solutions as an affine coset modulo the degree-4 vanishing product.

    Why valid: By the Factor Theorem, any polynomial vanishing at 0, 1, 2, 3 is a multiple of x(x-1)(x-2)(x-3).

    Result: p(x) = x^2 + 1 + x(x-1)(x-2)(x-3)q(x)

Result

Under deg(p) <= 3, the difference d(x) = p(x) - (x^2 + 1) has 4 distinct roots (0, 1, 2, 3) while having degree at most 3, forcing d(x) = 0 identically by the Polynomial Identity Theorem, so p(x) = x^2 + 1 uniquely. When the degree bound is removed, any polynomial matching the four data points satisfies p(x) = x^2 + 1 + x(x-1)(x-2)(x-3)q(x) for an arbitrary polynomial q(x) in R[x]. The inference that d(x) must be identically zero (and thus that p is unique) depended strictly on the degree bound <= 3.

Check

Verify at x = 0, 1, 2, 3: x(x-1)(x-2)(x-3) vanishes, leaving x^2 + 1 = 1, 2, 5, 10 for every q(x). At x = 4 with q(x) = 1, p(4) = 17 + 24 = 41 != 17, confirming non-uniqueness without bound.

Determine when finite data uniquely determine a polynomial and characterize all solutions when the degree bound is removed

Mistake clinic · diagnose and repair

A tempting wrong path

  • A polynomial matching n data points is always unique without requiring a degree bound.
  • Agreement at three points proves that two functions are identical.

Diagnose

  • If p(x) fits 4 data points, does that automatically mean p(x) must be a quadratic or cubic?
  • Can two different degree-3 polynomials agree at three distinct values?

Repair

  • Uniqueness requires deg(p) <= n - 1; without a degree bound, infinitely many higher-degree polynomials match the same points.
  • Agreement at 3 points proves identity only for polynomials of degree at most 2. For degree 3, they differ by c*(x-x1)(x-x2)(x-3).

1-2-3 quick check

  1. 1 · CheckCHECK: Prove why 4 points uniquely fix a cubic polynomial but leave a quartic polynomial underdetermined.
  2. 2 · ApplyAPPLY: If p and q have degree <= 3 and agree at -1, 0, 1 with p(2)-q(2)=12, find p(3)-q(3).
  3. 3 · ConnectCONNECT: Refute the heuristic that agreement at 3 inputs always proves an identity.

Now you do one

Polynomials f and g have degree at most 3. They agree at x = 1, 2, 3, and f(4) - g(4) = 18. Find f(0) - g(0).

Root Multiplicity Parity, Sign Trapping, and Minimal Degree

1

Deduce root multiplicities from sign behavior across zeros, prove degrees 1-4 impossible via IVT and quadratic sign constraints, and construct a verified degree-5 polynomial

Hard concept
Section 2 of 3← PreviousNext →

Conditions for this construction

  • Factor Theorem and definition of root multiplicity.
  • Continuity of real polynomials.
Prerequisite reference
  • Evaluate degree bounds, polynomial identity theorem, interpolation uniqueness, and unbounded polynomial cosets
Why this section matters for the question set

This unit builds toward

  • Q3 · D4 · the hardest question in this set

  • Q9 · D4
    The move learners miss: Prove that x^2 + bx - 1 is never irreducible: discriminant Delta = b^2 + 4 > 0 for all real b. Try it in Core2
  1. Connect sign variation across zeros to multiplicity parity: odd multiplicity crosses the axis; even multiplicity is tangent without sign change.
    Why valid: Near root r of multiplicity m, p(x) ~ k(x-r)^m, whose sign flips iff m is odd.
    Result: multiplicity parity criteria
  2. Apply the Intermediate Value Theorem to trap root signs: positive values on both sides of a zero force even multiplicity to prevent intermediate zeros.
    Why valid: If a simple zero changed sign between two positive evaluations, IVT would force another zero, violating given root count.
    Result: IVT sign-trapping constraint
  3. The step the hard question turns onEvaluate quadratic factor irreducibility: an irreducible quadratic has negative discriminant and constant sign on R.
    Why valid: If Delta = b^2 - 4ac < 0, ax^2 + bx + c never vanishes and has the constant sign of a.
    Result: constant-sign quadratic constraint
  4. Construct explicit minimal-degree polynomial satisfying all constraints and prove all smaller degrees impossible.
    Why valid: Systematic elimination of degrees 0, 1, 2, 3, 4 establishes degree 5 as the exact minimal degree.
    Result: minimal degree proof and verified specimen

The key step

A real polynomial changes sign across a real root if and only if its multiplicity is odd; if a polynomial is positive on both sides of an isolated zero, the root must have even multiplicity (at least 2), and any quadratic factor with no real roots maintains a strictly constant sign.

Formula reference · meaning and conditions

Equations and validity

EquationWhat it tells youWhen you can use it

\lim_{x \to r^+} \operatorname{sgn}(p(x)) = (-1)^m \lim_{x \to r^-} \operatorname{sgn}(p(x))

A polynomial changes sign across an isolated real root r if and only if its multiplicity m is odd; if m is even, the sign is preserved.

  • p(x) is a real polynomial.
  • r is an isolated root.

\Delta = b^2 - 4ac < 0 \implies ax^2 + bx + c \text{ has constant sign on } \mathbb{R}

A real quadratic with negative discriminant has no real roots and maintains a constant non-zero sign across all real numbers.

  • a != 0, a, b, c in R.
  • Discriminant strictly negative.

Worked explanation

Q3 · Owner-supplied question, verbatim

Find the least possible degree of a real polynomial whose distinct real zeros are exactly $-1$ and $1$, and which satisfies $p(0)=p(2)=1$. Exhibit one such polynomial, verify that it has no other real zeros, and prove that every smaller degree is impossible. You may use continuity of real polynomial functions and the factor theorem. This is an advanced competition extension.

Follow the operation, its justification and its result.

  1. Eliminate degrees <= 2: degree 0 and 1 lack two distinct roots; degree 2 gives p(x) = -(x^2-1) which evaluates to p(2) = -3 != 1.

    Why valid: A quadratic with roots +/-1 is c(x^2-1); p(0)=1 fixes c=-1, yielding p(2) = -3.

    Result: degrees 0, 1, 2 impossible

  2. Apply IVT to eliminate degree 3: p(0)=1>0 and p(2)=1>0 with zero at 1 forces mult(1) >= 2, leaving only c(x-1)^2(x+1) which gives p(2)=3 != 1.

    Why valid: If mult(1) were 1, p(x) would change sign across 1, and by IVT would have a zero in (1,2), contradicting that roots are only +/-1.

    Result: degree 3 impossible

  3. Eliminate degree 4: all-real-roots cases give p(2)/p(0) in {9, -3, -27} != 1; while an irreducible quadratic factor has constant sign, forcing p(0) and p(2) to have opposite signs.

    Why valid: With quadratic factor q(x), p(x) = c(x^2-1)q(x). At x=0, x^2-1=-1; at x=2, x^2-1=3. If q has constant sign, p(0) and p(2) have opposite signs, contradicting p(0)=1 and p(2)=1.

    Result: degree 4 impossible

  4. Construct degree 5 polynomial p(x) = k(x-1)^2(x+1)(x^2 + bx + c), solve for parameters, and verify discriminant is negative.

    Why valid: Setting k=1/4, c=4, b=-10/3 yields p(x) = (1/12)(x-1)^2(x+1)(3x^2 - 10x + 12). Discriminant Delta = 100 - 144 = -44 < 0 proves no other real zeros.

    Result: explicit degree 5 solution verified

Result

The least possible degree is 5. Degrees 0 and 1 cannot have two distinct zeros. Degree 2 gives p(x) = c(x^2-1) with p(0)=1 => c=-1 => p(2)=-3 != 1. Degree 3: since p(0)=1>0 and p(2)=1>0 while p(1)=0, if mult(1)=1, p would change sign across 1, requiring an additional real zero in (1,2) by the Intermediate Value Theorem; thus mult(1) >= 2, mult(-1) >= 1, giving p(x) = c(x-1)^2(x+1), but p(0)=1 => c=1 => p(2)=3 != 1. Degree 4: if mult(1)=2, mult(-1)=1 requires a linear factor introducing a third real zero; if mult(1)=mult(-1)=2, c(x^2-1)^2 gives p(2)/p(0)=9 != 1; and an irreducible quadratic factor q(x) has constant sign on R, forcing p(0) and p(2) to have opposite signs when multiplied by x^2-1, contradicting p(0)=p(2)=1. For degree 5, p(x) = (1/12)(x-1)^2(x+1)(3x^2 - 10x + 12) satisfies p(0)=1, p(2)=1, real zeros exactly +/-1, and discriminant Delta = 100 - 144 = -44 < 0 guarantees no other real zeros.

Check

Check p(0) = (1/12)(1)(1)(12) = 1. Check p(2) = (1/12)(1)(3)(3*4 - 20 + 12) = (1/12)(3)(4) = 1. Check zeros: x = 1 (mult 2), x = -1 (mult 1). 3x^2 - 10x + 12 = 3(x - 5/3)^2 + 11/3 > 0 for all real x. Degree is 2 + 1 + 2 = 5.

Mistake clinic · diagnose and repair

A tempting wrong path

  • Every zero of a polynomial must be a simple zero (multiplicity 1).
  • Any four given values can always be fitted by a degree 3 or degree 4 polynomial with prescribed real zeros.

Diagnose

  • Can a polynomial touch the x-axis and turn around without crossing it?
  • If a quadratic factor has no real roots, can it change sign between x = 0 and x = 2?

Repair

  • A root has even multiplicity (2, 4, ...) when the curve is tangent to the axis without crossing; crossing with sign change occurs only for odd multiplicities.
  • An irreducible quadratic has constant sign across all real numbers. When multiplied by (x^2 - 1), it forces p(0) and p(2) to have opposite signs, making degree 4 impossible.

1-2-3 quick check

  1. 1 · CheckCHECK: Prove why p(0)=1>0, p(2)=1>0, and p(1)=0 prevents 1 from being a root of multiplicity 1.
  2. 2 · ApplyAPPLY: If p is monic with distinct real zeros strictly +/-1 and p(0)=1, prove no irreducible quadratic factor can be present.
  3. 3 · ConnectCONNECT: Verify that 3x^2 - 10x + 12 has discriminant -44 < 0 and is strictly positive on all of R.

Worked explanation

Q4 · Owner-supplied question, verbatim

For real $a$, consider $F_a(x)=(a-1)x^4+2(a-1)x^3+(a+2)x^2+2(a+2)x$. Develop a complete classification of its degree and number of distinct real zeros as $a$ varies. Identify all exceptional parameter values where the degree drops or zeros coincide, and account for multiplicities at those values. Justify that your cases are exhaustive.

Follow the operation, its justification and its result.

  1. Group and factor F_a(x) into x(x+2)[(a-1)x^2 + (a+2)].

    Why valid: (a-1)x^3(x+2) + (a+2)x(x+2) = x(x+2)[(a-1)x^2 + (a+2)].

    Result: factorized form with fixed roots 0, -2

  2. Identify degree drop at leading coefficient zero: a = 1.

    Why valid: When a = 1, the x^4 and x^3 terms vanish; F_1(x) = 3x^2 + 6x has degree 2 with roots 0 and -2.

    Result: degree drops to 2, 2 distinct zeros

  3. Analyze roots of quadratic factor x^2 = (a+2)/(1-a) for a != 1.

    Why valid: Sign of (a+2)/(1-a) determines whether new real roots exist: < 0 on (-inf, -2) U (1, inf); = 0 at a = -2; > 0 on (-2, 1).

    Result: intervals for 0 vs 2 additional real roots

  4. Check for root coincidences where +/-sqrt((a+2)/(1-a)) equals 0 or -2.

    Why valid: Coincides with 0 at a = -2 (giving triple zero at 0). Coincides with -2 when (a+2)/(1-a) = 4 => a = 2/5 (giving double zero at -2).

    Result: exceptional points a = -2 and a = 2/5 identified with multiplicities

Result

F_a(x) factors as x(x+2)[(a-1)x^2 + (a+2)]. At a = 1, leading coefficient vanishes, degree drops from 4 to 2, and F_1(x) = 3x(x+2) with 2 distinct real zeros (0, -2, mult 1 each). For a != 1, degree is 4. The quadratic factor gives x^2 = (a+2)/(1-a). For a in (-inf, -2) U (1, inf), (a+2)/(1-a) < 0, giving 2 distinct real zeros (0, -2, mult 1 each). At a = -2, (a+2)/(1-a) = 0, giving 2 distinct real zeros: 0 (mult 3) and -2 (mult 1). For a in (-2, 1), (a+2)/(1-a) > 0. At exceptional value a = 2/5, x^2 = 4 so roots are +/-2; the root -2 coincides with existing root, giving 3 distinct real zeros: 0 (mult 1), 2 (mult 1), -2 (mult 2). For a in (-2, 1) \ {2/5}, all 4 roots (0, -2, +/-sqrt((a+2)/(1-a))) are distinct (4 zeros, mult 1 each). Cases are exhaustive by sign and boundary analysis of (a-1) and (a+2)/(1-a).

Check

Verify exceptional points: at a = 1, F_1(x) = 3x(x+2) (deg 2, zeros 0, -2). At a = -2, F_-2(x) = -3x^3(x+2) (deg 4, zeros 0 mult 3, -2 mult 1). At a = 2/5, F_0.4(x) = -0.6 x(x+2)(x^2 - 4) = -0.6 x(x+2)^2(x-2) (deg 4, zeros 0 mult 1, 2 mult 1, -2 mult 2). All cases exhaustive.

Mistake clinic · diagnose and repair

A tempting wrong path

  • The largest printed exponent is always the degree of a polynomial.
  • Cancelling common factors in a rational expression makes it a polynomial function on all real numbers.

Diagnose

  • What is the degree of (a - 1)x^4 + 3x^2 when a = 1?
  • Is (x^2 - 1)/(x - 1) defined at x = 1?

Repair

  • The degree is the highest exponent with a non-zero coefficient. If a parameter causes the leading coefficient to vanish, the degree drops.
  • Algebraic cancellation is valid only on the original domain where the denominator is non-zero. A removable singularity requires a continuous extension.

  3. 3 · ConnectCONNECT: Explain why R_1(x) = (x-1)^2/(x-1) has no real zeros on its original domain.
