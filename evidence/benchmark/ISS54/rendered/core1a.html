The key step

Why printed exponents do not determine degree when coefficients vanish, and why the division identity p(x) = (x-a)q(x) + R directly forces R = p(a).

Worked explanation

Q1 · Owner-supplied question, verbatim

For $p_k(x)=(k-2)x^3+3x^2-x+4$, where $k$ is real, classify the degree when $k=2$ and when $k\neq2$. Explain why the highest printed exponent is not sufficient to decide the degree.

Follow the operation, its justification and its result.

  1. Examine the leading term (k - 2)x^3 and test the coefficient condition k = 2.

    Why valid: A term with coefficient zero contributes zero to the polynomial value for all x.

    Result: For k = 2, (k-2) = 0, so the x^3 term vanishes, reducing p_2(x) to 3x^2 - x + 4.

  2. Classify the degree for both parameter cases k = 2 and k != 2.

    Why valid: Degree is the maximum exponent among terms having non-zero coefficients.

    Result: Degree is 2 when k = 2, and degree is 3 when k != 2.

  3. Explain why the printed visual exponent alone fails to establish degree.

    Why valid: Formally, deg(p) = max{n : a_n != 0}. A printed symbol string does not account for coefficient cancellation.

    Result: The highest printed exponent may have a vanishing coefficient (0*x^3), which makes it a non-leading term.

Result

When k=2, (k-2)=0, so p_2(x) = 3x^2 - x + 4, which has degree 2. When k != 2, (k-2) != 0, so the leading term is (k-2)x^3 and the degree is 3. The highest printed exponent is not sufficient because polynomial degree is defined as the highest power with a non-zero coefficient; if the coefficient evaluates to zero, that term vanishes from the polynomial sum.

Check

Verify by testing specific values: at k=2, p_2(1) = 3(1)^2 - 1 + 4 = 6 matches quadratic evaluation; at k=3, p_3(x) = x^3 + 3x^2 - x + 4 is strictly cubic.

Diagnose

Repair

  • When k = 2, the coefficient (k - 2) equals 0, so 0*x^3 vanishes from the sum. The highest power with a non-zero coefficient is x^2, making the degree 2.

  1. Identify domain exclusions where rational denominators vanish.
    Why valid: Division by zero is undefined in real arithmetic.
    Result: Domain is restricted to real numbers where the denominator is non-zero.
  2. Distinguish between algebraic simplification and function identity across real numbers.
    Why valid: Two functions are equal iff their domains match and their values agree at all domain points.
    Result: Simplification is valid only on the punctured domain; functions with different domains are not equal.
  3. The step the hard question turns onReconstruct polynomials from given zeros and scale-fixing point values.
    Why valid: Factors (x - r) fix zeros, while a point evaluation uniquely determines the leading multiplier a.
    Result: Unique quadratic polynomial satisfying zeros and point conditions.

The key step

Why algebraic cancellation (x-1)/(x-1) = 1 is invalid at x = 1, creating a removable singularity hole that prevents function identity across all real numbers.

Worked explanation

Q6 · Owner-supplied question, verbatim

Compare the original expression $r(x)=(x^2-1)/(x-1)$ with $q(x)=x+1$. State the domain of $r$, simplify it where valid, and decide whether $r$ and $q$ are the same function on all real numbers. Address $x=1$ explicitly.

Follow the operation, its justification and its result.

  1. Determine the domain of the rational expression r(x) by setting denominator != 0.

    Why valid: Division by zero is undefined in real arithmetic.

    Result: Denominator x - 1 = 0 when x = 1, so Domain(r) = R \ {1}.

  2. Simplify r(x) by factorising the numerator and cancelling the common factor for x in Domain(r).

    Why valid: For any x != 1, (x - 1)/(x - 1) = 1.

    Result: r(x) = x + 1 for all x != 1.

  3. Evaluate both functions at x = 1 and apply the formal criterion for function equality.

    Why valid: Two functions f and g are equal iff Domain(f) = Domain(g) and f(x) = g(x) for all x in Domain.

    Result: r(1) is undefined, but q(1) = 2. Since their domains differ, r and q are not the same function on all real numbers.

Result

The domain of r is R \ {1} (all real numbers x != 1) because division by zero is undefined. For x != 1, x - 1 != 0, so simplifying gives r(x) = (x-1)(x+1)/(x-1) = x + 1. However, r and q are NOT the same function on all real numbers because two functions are identical if and only if they share the exact same domain and agree at every point. At x = 1, q(1) = 1 + 1 = 2, but r(1) = 0/0 is undefined; therefore r has a removable singularity (hole) at (1, 2) while q is continuous across all R.

Check

Evaluate left and right limits: lim_{x -> 1} r(x) = 2 = q(1), confirming the singularity at x=1 is removable, but the function value r(1) does not exist.

Mistake clinic · diagnose and repair

A tempting wrong path

  • Assuming that simplifying (x^2 - 1)/(x - 1) to x + 1 means both expressions represent the same function on all real numbers.

Diagnose

  • What is the value of r(1) = (1^2 - 1)/(1 - 1)? Can a function be defined where division by zero occurs?

Repair

  • At x = 1, r(1) yields 0/0, which is undefined. The function q(x) = x + 1 is defined at x = 1 with q(1) = 2. Two functions cannot be equal on R if their domains differ.
