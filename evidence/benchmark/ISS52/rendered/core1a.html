Polynomial inference under constraints

Learning promise

Advanced competition extensions of Grade 9 polynomial foundations: finite-data identification, multiplicity/sign, exact zero sets, parameter cases, modeling, and domain preservation.

Concept route

  1. What finite polynomial data actually determine

  2. Multiplicity, sign and exact real-zero sets
    1. Local sign behaviour determines only the parity of a root's multiplicity; the degree budget and other constraints are what turn those parities into exact multiplicities.
  3. Proving a least degree under an exact zero-set constraint
    1. A construction proves possibility only; minimality needs a lower-bound argument that eliminates every smaller degree while respecting the exact real-zero set.
  4. Exhaustive parameter case splits from structural events
    1. Do not start by sampling parameter values. First derive every equality or sign boundary at which degree, root existence or root coincidence can change; those events determine the exhaustive case split.
  5. Geometry-derived models versus finite interpolation
    1. Matching a finite table is evidence of agreement at those inputs, not a license to replace a model derived from the mechanism and its domain.
  6. Cancellation, original domains and polynomial extensions
    1. Cancelling a common factor simplifies the rule only where the cancelled denominator was nonzero; assigning a value at the hole is a separate extension operation.
  7. Qualified rules instead of unconditional polynomial slogans
    1. Attach the exact hidden hypothesis to each familiar polynomial/rational-expression rule before applying it.
  8. What survives when a degree bound is removed
    1. Tag every conclusion by the hypothesis that actually forces it; after removing the degree bound, construct the complete multiplier freedom before reclassifying claims.
  9. Identify a difference without identifying its components
    1. Move the observations to the difference polynomial, identify that bounded object, then test component uniqueness with a common additive perturbation.
  10. Prove an exact real-zero-set list is complete
    1. Treat the named zeros as required factors, enumerate multiplicity and residual-factor budgets, and eliminate every residual case that would introduce another real zero.

  1. Separate observed values from structural assumptions such as degree, parity or domain.
    Why valid: A conclusion is licensed only by the conditions actually stated.
    Result: A constraint ledger that distinguishes observations from bounds.
  2. Replace agreement of two polynomials by zeros of their difference.
    Why valid: p(c)=q(c) exactly means (p-q)(c)=0.

The key step

Finite agreement becomes an identity only after a structural bound makes too many distinct roots impossible for the difference polynomial.

Worked explanation

A real polynomial r has degree at most two and r(0)=2, r(1)=3, r(2)=6. Show what is forced by the degree bound, then describe what changes if that bound is removed.

Follow the operation, its justification and its result.

  1. Check x^2+2 at the three inputs; the difference has three roots. A nonzero degree-at-most-two difference is impossible. Without the bound, factor all three roots from the difference.

Result

With degree at most two, r(x)=x^2+2 uniquely. Without the bound, r(x)=x^2+2+x(x-1)(x-2)h(x) for any real polynomial h.

Check

Substitute 0,1,2 and test h=1 as a distinct unbounded example.

Finite agreement becomes an identity only after a structural bound makes too many distinct roots impossible for the difference polynomial.

Repair

  • No. Their difference may be x(x-1)(x-2)h(x). Identity follows only when an applicable degree bound makes that many distinct roots impossible.

Why this section matters for the question set

This unit builds toward

  1. At each stated zero, record whether the polynomial changes sign or keeps the same nonzero sign locally.
    Why valid: The local sign condition is evidence about multiplicity parity, not about scale.
    Result: Odd/even parity requirements at each zero.
  2. Assign the least positive multiplicities with those parities, then compare their sum with the degree bound.
    Why valid: Each multiplicity contributes to total degree.
    Result: The remaining degree budget, if any.
  3. Add monicity/value constraints and account for any residual factor that the degree budget permits.
    Why valid: Residual factors may introduce forbidden real zeros even when the named factors are correct.
    Result: A finite candidate list or a unique factorization.
  4. The step the hard question turns onCheck sign behaviour, exact zero set and every value/leading-coefficient condition separately.
    Why valid: Correct factors alone do not prove completeness.
    Result: Verified multiplicities and no omitted real-zero source.

The key step

Local sign behaviour determines only the parity of a root's multiplicity; the degree budget and other constraints are what turn those parities into exact multiplicities.

Worked explanation

A real polynomial has zeros at -1,0,1, changes sign at ±1, and keeps the same nonzero sign on both sides of 0. If its degree is at most four, what multiplicities are forced before any scale condition is used?

Follow the operation, its justification and its result.

  1. Translate sign change to odd multiplicity and no sign change to even multiplicity. The least positive choices already total four, so the degree bound leaves no room for larger multiplicities.

Result

The forced multiplicities are 1 at -1, 2 at 0, and 1 at 1.

Check

The factor (x+1)x^2(x-1) flips sign at ±1 and not at 0.

Local sign behaviour determines only the parity of a root's multiplicity; the degree budget and other constraints are what turn those parities into exact multiplicities.

Mistake clinic · diagnose and repair

A tempting wrong path

  • Every named zero is simple unless the stem explicitly says repeated.

Diagnose

  • If a continuous polynomial has the same nonzero sign immediately to both sides of a zero, can that zero be simple?

Repair

  • No. A simple (odd-multiplicity) root changes sign. Same-sign crossing data require even multiplicity.

1-2-3 quick check

  1. 1 · CheckCHECK: State which multiplicity parity changes sign at a real zero and which preserves the local nonzero sign.
  2. 2 · ApplyAPPLY: For changed roots -3, 0, 3 with sign changes only at ±3 and degree at most 4, determine the forced multiplicity pattern before choosing a scale.
  3. 3 · ConnectCONNECT: Explain why multiplicity bookkeeping is also necessary when an exact distinct-real-zero set must be proved complete.

Proving a least degree under an exact zero-set constraint

1

A construction proves possibility only; minimality needs a lower-bound argument that eliminates every smaller degree while respecting the exact real-zero set.

Medium concept
Section 3 of 10← PreviousNext →

Conditions for this construction

  • Can factor routine one-variable polynomials and use the factor theorem.
  • Can distinguish a stated domain/degree bound from an inferred property.
Why this section matters for the question set

This unit builds toward

  • Q3 · D4
    The move learners miss: Eliminate total degree 4 using continuity, exact-zero-set restrictions, and the two value conditions on the quadratic g. Try it in Core2
  1. Factor all required zeros first and translate the remaining value constraints to an auxiliary polynomial.
    Why valid: This isolates where extra roots could appear.
    Result: An auxiliary factor with explicit value/sign constraints.
  2. Turn each proposed total degree into a maximum degree for the auxiliary factor.
    Why valid: The required factors already consume part of the degree.
    Result: A finite sequence of smaller-degree cases to eliminate.
  3. Use value equations, continuity and quadratic discriminant/sign facts to show why each smaller auxiliary degree creates a contradiction or an extra real zero.
    Why valid: Those tools address the exact-zero-set condition, not merely interpolation.
    Result: A genuine lower bound on total degree.
  4. The step the hard question turns onOnly after the lower bound is closed, build one polynomial at that degree and check that any remaining factor has no additional real zeros.
    Why valid: Possibility plus the lower bound proves minimality.
    Result: A minimal-degree witness with exact zero set.

The key step

A construction proves possibility only; minimality needs a lower-bound argument that eliminates every smaller degree while respecting the exact real-zero set.

Worked explanation

After factoring two allowed zeros from a candidate polynomial, an auxiliary factor g is known to satisfy g(-1)<0 and g(1)>0. Explain why a proposed quadratic g whose only allowed real zero in that interval would have to be a repeated zero cannot satisfy both sign conditions.

Follow the operation, its justification and its result.

  1. Use continuity to force at least one real zero between -1 and 1. If the quadratic has only one real zero, its discriminant is zero and the root is double, so the sign does not switch. That contradicts the endpoint signs.

Result

A quadratic with only one real zero has that zero with even multiplicity and therefore does not change sign across it; the opposite endpoint signs require a sign-changing real zero.

Check

Compare with (x-c)^2, which is nonnegative on both sides of c.

A construction proves possibility only; minimality needs a lower-bound argument that eliminates every smaller degree while respecting the exact real-zero set.

Mistake clinic · diagnose and repair

A tempting wrong path

  • Once a low-degree example is found, no separate proof that smaller degrees fail is needed.

Diagnose

  • If you exhibit a degree-5 polynomial satisfying all conditions, have you proved 5 is least?

Repair

  • No. You must eliminate degrees below 5. The exact-zero-set condition often makes continuity or discriminant arguments the missing lower-bound step.

1-2-3 quick check

  1. 1 · CheckCHECK: Separate the two obligations in a least-degree claim: rule out every smaller degree, then verify one witness.
  2. 2 · ApplyAPPLY: If an auxiliary quadratic has opposite signs at two inputs, use continuity and multiplicity to decide whether a lone double root could account for the sign change.
  3. 3 · ConnectCONNECT: Relate the same residual-factor check to proving that an exact real-zero-set classification has omitted no polynomial.

The key step

Do not start by sampling parameter values. First derive every equality or sign boundary at which degree, root existence or root coincidence can change; those events determine the exhaustive case split.

Worked explanation

For G_t(x)=x(x+3)((t-2)x^2+(t+1)), derive every parameter value at which the degree or distinct real-zero pattern can change, then classify the parameter line.

Follow the operation, its justification and its result.

  1. Factorization is already exposed. Degree drops when t-2=0. Extra real roots require (t+1)/(2-t)>0, with boundary t=-1. Collision with -3 occurs when (t+1)/(2-t)=9, giving t=17/10. Order the events and test each interval; refactor at each event.

Result

Critical values are t=-1, 17/10, 2. For t<-1 and t>2 there are two distinct simple real zeros 0,-3. At t=-1 there are two distinct zeros with 0 of multiplicity 3. Check

Choose t=0, t=19/10 and t=3 as interval checks, then substitute each exceptional t directly.

Do not start by sampling parameter values. First derive every equality or sign boundary at which degree, root existence or root coincidence can change; those events determine the exhaustive case split.

Repair

  1. Write the physical dimensions and derive the model from the construction before fitting any formula.
    Why valid: The mechanism determines how the quantity depends on the input.
    Result: A geometry-derived formula.
  2. Derive the admissible domain from positive physical dimensions and keep limiting configurations separate.

The key step

Matching a finite table is evidence of agreement at those inputs, not a license to replace a model derived from the mechanism and its domain.

Worked explanation

A 12 cm square sheet is cut by x cm corner squares to form an open box. A quadratic happens to match the true volumes at three chosen x-values. What information must be checked before treating that quadratic as the volume model?

Follow the operation, its justification and its result.

  1. The geometry fixes height x and base side 12-2x. The domain comes from positive dimensions. A finite set of matches can be shared by different polynomials, so a new domain-valid input or algebraic difference is needed.

Result

Derive V(x)=x(12-2x)^2 from the geometry, state 0<x<6 for a non-degenerate box, then compare the candidate formula at an additional admissible x. Three matching rows alone do not identify the geometric law.

Check

Check that the geometric volume remains nonnegative throughout 0<x<6 and compare a changed input.

Matching a finite table is evidence of agreement at those inputs, not a license to replace a model derived from the mechanism and its domain.

Repair

  • No. Derive the model from the mechanism or impose a justified structural bound; otherwise a nonzero polynomial can vanish at all sampled inputs.

Conditions for this construction

  • Can factor routine one-variable polynomials and use the factor theorem.

Why this section matters for the question set

This unit builds toward

  • Q6 · D2
    The move learners miss: Classify whether x=1 is an allowed zero as a varies. Try it in Core2
  • Q10 · D4
    The move learners miss: Replace each slogan by a conditional rule before touching the examples. Try it in Core2
  1. Record the original domain before simplifying a rational expression.
    Why valid: A function includes both a rule and its admissible inputs.
    Result: An explicit excluded-value set.
  2. Factor and cancel only under the nonzero condition already recorded.
    Why valid: Algebraic cancellation is valid where the cancelled factor is nonzero.
    Result: A simpler rule with the original domain unchanged.
  3. Classify zeros by requiring both rule value zero and membership in the original domain.
    Why valid: An excluded input cannot be a zero of a function that is undefined there.
    Result: The original function's zero set.
  4. The step the hard question turns onDescribe any all-real polynomial with the same rule as an extension, and say exactly what new value it supplies.
    Why valid: Extension changes the domain intentionally rather than retroactively changing the original function.
    Result: Original function and polynomial extension kept distinct.

The key step

Cancelling a common factor simplifies the rule only where the cancelled denominator was nonzero; assigning a value at the hole is a separate extension operation.

Worked explanation

Let H_b(x)=[x^2-(b+4)x+4b]/(x-b) on its original domain x≠b. Simplify on that domain, classify whether x=4 is a zero as b varies, and describe the polynomial extension.

Follow the operation, its justification and its result.

  1. Factor the numerator as (x-b)(x-4), cancel only for x≠b, then test whether the candidate zero belongs to the original domain. Extension is a separate operation that fills the hole.

Result

H_b(x)=x-4 for x≠b. If b≠4, x=4 is an allowed zero; if b=4 it is excluded and H_b has no zero. The polynomial extension is x-4 on all real numbers.

Check

At b=4 the simplified formula is zero at 4 but the original function is undefined there; this boundary case detects domain loss.

Cancelling a common factor simplifies the rule only where the cancelled denominator was nonzero; assigning a value at the hole is a separate extension operation.

Mistake clinic · diagnose and repair

A tempting wrong path

  • After cancellation, the excluded point automatically returns to the function's domain.

Diagnose

  • If (x-c) cancels and the simplified formula is zero at x=c, is c automatically a zero of the original rational function?

Repair

  • No. Check the original domain first. The simplified polynomial can define an extension, but that is a different function.

  1. 1 · CheckCHECK: After cancelling a factor, restate the original excluded input before naming any zero of the function.
  2. 2 · ApplyAPPLY: Simplify (x-c)(x+2)/(x-c) on x≠c and classify whether -2 is a zero when c=-2 and when c is different from -2.

What survives when a degree bound is removed

Subject: MathematicsTopic: Polynomials in one real variableConcept: What survives when a degree bound is removedConcept difficulty: Medium concept
1

Tag every conclusion by the hypothesis that actually forces it; after removing the degree bound, construct the complete multiplier freedom before reclassifying claims.

Medium concept
Section 8 of 10← PreviousNext →

Conditions for this construction

  • Can factor routine one-variable polynomials and use the factor theorem.
  • Can preserve stated degree and domain conditions while transforming an expression.
Why this section matters for the question set

This unit builds toward

  • Q7 · D3
    The move learners miss: Remove the degree bound by parameterizing every even multiplier that preserves p(0)=4. Try it in Core2
  1. Separate consequences of evenness and fixed values from consequences that use the degree bound.
    Why valid: Removing one hypothesis invalidates only inferences that depended on it.
    Result: A dependency ledger for the bounded conclusions.
  2. Use evenness to reflect the required roots and factor all roots forced before the degree bound is used.
    Why valid: Evenness gives p(-r)=p(r), so every nonzero real root appears with its negative partner.
    Result: A forced even factor independent of the degree ceiling.
  3. In the bounded case, spend the degree budget and use the value at zero to determine the remaining scalar.
    Why valid: Once the forced distinct roots consume the allowed degree, no nonconstant residual factor remains.
    Result: The unique bounded polynomial and its bounded claim statuses.
  4. The step the hard question turns onAfter removing the bound, parameterize even multipliers that preserve the value at zero and use contrasting multipliers to test necessity.
    Why valid: A complete admissible family supplies direct counterexamples to claims that were degree-bound dependent.
    Result: A necessary/possible/impossible classification that changes only where the removed hypothesis mattered.

The key step

Tag every conclusion by the hypothesis that actually forces it; after removing the degree bound, construct the complete multiplier freedom before reclassifying claims.

Worked explanation

A real even polynomial r has degree at most four, r(1)=r(3)=0, and r(0)=9. Determine what the degree bound forces. Then remove only the degree bound and describe a family that preserves the stated conditions.

Follow the operation, its justification and its result.

  1. Evenness supplies the negative partners. Four distinct roots consume the bounded degree, and r(0)=9 fixes the scalar. After removing the bound, an even multiplier with value 1 at zero preserves evenness, the named roots and r(0).

Result
