Motion in Two Dimensions — Study Book

Motion in a plane is two straight-line motions happening together on a shared clock. This book teaches you to split any motion into perpendicular parts, solve each part with the tools you already know from one-dimensional motion, and then join the parts back into one answer.

How to use this book: read the key idea, study the explanation and the picture, follow the worked example line by line, then rebuild the idea in your own words before you try the practice questions. Always write your attempt before opening a hint or an answer; the comparison is where the learning happens.

Position and displacement in a plane

Key idea

A position in a plane needs two numbers, x and y. Displacement is the arrow from the start point to the end point, found by subtracting coordinates.

Choose an origin and two perpendicular axes. Every point then has coordinates (x, y), and the position vector is the arrow from the origin to that point.

4

Understand it

Choose an origin and two perpendicular axes. Every point then has coordinates (x, y), and the position vector is the arrow from the origin to that point.

Displacement is the change in position: Δx = x₂ − x₁ and Δy = y₂ − y₁. Its magnitude is √(Δx² + Δy²), and it does not depend on the path actually followed between the two points.

Distance travelled counts every metre of the path, so it is never smaller than the magnitude of displacement.

Common slip: Adding the path lengths gives distance, not displacement. Displacement only compares the start and end points.

5

Worked example

A robot moves from (1 m, 1 m) to (4 m, 5 m). Find its displacement.

  1. Subtract the x-coordinates to get the horizontal part: Δx = 4 − 1 = 3 m.
  2. Subtract the y-coordinates to get the vertical part: Δy = 5 − 1 = 4 m.
  3. Join the two perpendicular parts with Pythagoras: |Δr| = √(3² + 4²) = 5 m.
  4. State the direction as well: tan θ = 4/3, so θ ≈ 53° above the x-axis.

Result: 5 m at about 53° above the x-axis.

6

Rebuild it yourself

Explain in your own words why two different paths between the same two points have the same displacement.

Compare with a model answer

Displacement is defined only by the start and end positions: Δr = r₂ − r₁. The path does not appear in the formula, so every path between the same two points gives the same arrow.

Practice

A particle moves from point A (2 m, 3 m) to point B (8 m, 11 m). Find the magnitude of its displacement.

Hint

Subtract coordinates first.

Worked answer
  1. Δx = 8 − 2 = 6 m and Δy = 11 − 3 = 8 m.
  2. |Δr| = √(6² + 8²) = 10 m.

Answer: 10 m

A student walks 30 m east and then 40 m north in 50 s. Find the magnitude of the displacement and the average velocity.

Hint

The two legs are perpendicular.

Worked answer
  1. Displacement = √(30² + 40²) = 50 m.
  2. Average velocity = displacement / time = 50 / 50 = 1 m/s, directed about 53° north of east.

Answer: 50 m; 1 m/s at about 53° north of east

Velocity and acceleration as vectors

Key idea

Velocity has an x-part and a y-part. Speed is the size of the velocity vector, and acceleration is the change in the velocity vector per second.

If x and y change with time, the velocity components are vₓ = Δx/Δt and v_y = Δy/Δt. The velocity vector joins them, and its size is the speed √(vₓ² + v_y²).

8

Understand it

If x and y change with time, the velocity components are vₓ = Δx/Δt and v_y = Δy/Δt. The velocity vector joins them, and its size is the speed √(vₓ² + v_y²).

Acceleration is the rate of change of the whole velocity vector. A change of direction alone, with the same speed, is still a change in velocity and so needs an acceleration.

Always subtract velocity vectors component by component: Δv = v_final − v_initial.

Common slip: Subtracting the speeds (4 − 3 = 1) instead of subtracting the vectors gives a wrong change in velocity when the direction changes.

9

Worked example

A particle's velocity changes from (6 m/s, 0) to (0, 8 m/s) in 5 s. Find the average acceleration.

  1. Write the change in each component: Δvₓ = 0 − 6 = −6 m/s and Δv_y = 8 − 0 = 8 m/s.
  2. Find the size of the change in velocity: |Δv| = √(6² + 8²) = 10 m/s.
  3. Divide by the time taken: a = 10 / 5 = 2 m/s².

Result: 2 m/s², directed along (−6, 8).

10

Rebuild it yourself

A car goes round a bend at a steady 20 m/s. Explain whether it is accelerating.

Compare with a model answer

Yes. Its speed is constant but the direction of its velocity changes, so the velocity vector changes, and any change in velocity per second is an acceleration.

Practice

The position of a particle is x = 3t m and y = 4t m, with t in seconds. Find the speed of the particle.

Hint

Read off vₓ and v_y from how x and y change each second.

Worked answer
  1. x grows by 3 m every second, so vₓ = 3 m/s; y grows by 4 m every second, so v_y = 4 m/s.
  2. Speed = √(3² + 4²) = 5 m/s.

Answer: 5 m/s

A particle's velocity changes from 4 m/s along x to 3 m/s along y in 2 s. Find the magnitude of the average acceleration.

Hint

Subtract the velocity vectors, not the speeds.

Worked answer
  1. Δv = (0 − 4, 3 − 0) = (−4, 3) m/s, so |Δv| = 5 m/s.
  2. Average acceleration = 5 / 2 = 2.5 m/s².

Answer: 2.5 m/s²

Perpendicular components are independent

Key idea

Horizontal and vertical motions share the same clock but do not affect each other. Gravity changes only the vertical velocity.

When a ball moves under gravity alone, the horizontal velocity stays constant because no horizontal force acts, while the vertical velocity grows at g every second.

12

Understand it

When a ball moves under gravity alone, the horizontal velocity stays constant because no horizontal force acts, while the vertical velocity grows at g every second.

Because both parts run on the same clock, the time found from the vertical motion is the same time used in the horizontal motion. That shared time links the two parts.

This is why a dropped ball and a ball thrown sideways from the same height land together.

Common slip: A faster sideways throw does not keep the ball in the air longer; the fall time depends only on the vertical motion.

13

Worked example

A ball leaves a shelf 1.25 m high moving sideways at 2 m/s. How far from the shelf does it land? Take g = 10 m/s².

  1. Use only the vertical motion to find the time: 1.25 = ½ × 10 × t², so t² = 0.25 and t = 0.5 s.
  2. Use the same time in the horizontal motion, where velocity is constant: x = 2 × 0.5 = 1 m.
  3. Check: the horizontal speed was never used to find the time, as independence requires.

Result: 1 m from the shelf.

14

Rebuild it yourself

Explain why the time of fall for a horizontally thrown ball does not depend on how fast it was thrown.

Compare with a model answer

The time comes from the vertical motion alone: it starts with zero vertical velocity and accelerates at g through the same height. The horizontal speed is a separate, independent component, so it changes where the ball lands, not when.

Practice

Two balls are 20 m above the ground. Ball A is dropped and ball B is thrown horizontally at 5 m/s at the same instant. Which reaches the ground first, and when? Take g = 10 m/s².

Hint

Compare only the vertical motions.

Worked answer
  1. Both start with zero vertical velocity and fall 20 m: 20 = ½ × 10 × t².
  2. t² = 4, so t = 2 s for both; they land together.

Answer: They land together after 2 s.

A ball rolls off a table top at 3 m/s and lands 0.4 s later. Find the horizontal distance from the table edge to the landing point.

Hint

Horizontal velocity is constant.

Worked answer
  1. Horizontal velocity stays 3 m/s throughout the flight.
  2. Distance = 3 × 0.4 = 1.2 m.

Answer: 1.2 m

Constant acceleration in a plane

Key idea

With constant acceleration, apply the one-dimensional equations of motion separately to x and to y, using the same time t.

For each direction, v = u + at and s = ut + ½at² hold with that direction's own components of initial velocity and acceleration.

16

Understand it

For each direction, v = u + at and s = ut + ½at² hold with that direction's own components of initial velocity and acceleration.

Solve the x-equations and the y-equations separately, then combine the results into a position vector or a velocity vector.

Only the time is shared between the two directions; every other quantity carries its own component.

Common slip: Using the total acceleration in the x-equation mixes directions; each equation takes only its own component.

17

Worked example

A puck starts at the origin with velocity (2 m/s, 0) and acceleration (0, 3 m/s²). Find its position after 2 s.

  1. Horizontal: x = uₓt + ½aₓt² = 2 × 2 + 0 = 4 m.
  2. Vertical: y = u_yt + ½a_yt² = 0 + ½ × 3 × 4 = 6 m.
  3. Combine the two components into one position: (4 m, 6 m), at a distance √(16 + 36) ≈ 7.2 m from the origin.

Result: (4 m, 6 m).

18

Rebuild it yourself

Describe the steps you would follow to find the velocity of a body with constant acceleration in a plane at a given time.

Compare with a model answer

Split the initial velocity and the acceleration into x and y components, apply v = u + at to each direction with the same t, then combine the two velocity components using Pythagoras to get the speed and the direction.

Practice

A particle starts from rest at the origin with constant acceleration 2 m/s² along x and 1 m/s² along y. Find its position after 4 s.

Hint

Use s = ½at² for each direction.

Worked answer
  1. x = ½ × 2 × 4² = 16 m.
  2. y = ½ × 1 × 4² = 8 m, so the position is (16 m, 8 m).

Answer: (16 m, 8 m)

A particle has initial velocity 3 m/s along x and constant acceleration 2 m/s² along y. Find its speed at t = 2 s.

Hint

Only the y-velocity changes.

Worked answer
  1. vₓ stays 3 m/s; v_y = 0 + 2 × 2 = 4 m/s.
  2. Speed = √(3² + 4²) = 5 m/s.

Answer: 5 m/s

Projectiles: time of flight, height and range

Key idea

Split the launch velocity into u cos θ horizontally and u sin θ vertically. The vertical part decides the time and the height; the horizontal part, multiplied by the time, gives the range.

A projectile launched at speed u and angle θ has uₓ = u cos θ, which stays constant, and u_y = u sin θ, which falls by g every second until it reaches zero at the top.

20

Understand it

A projectile launched at speed u and angle θ has uₓ = u cos θ, which stays constant, and u_y = u sin θ, which falls by g every second until it reaches zero at the top.

The time to the top is u sin θ / g, so on level ground the time of flight is T = 2u sin θ / g. The maximum height is H = (u sin θ)² / 2g.

The range is the horizontal velocity multiplied by the time of flight: R = u cos θ × T = u² sin 2θ / g, which is largest at 45°.

Common slip: At the top the velocity is not zero; only the vertical component is zero, and the horizontal component continues.

21

Worked example

A ball is kicked at 10 m/s at 30° above level ground. Find the time of flight and the range. Take g = 10 m/s².

  1. Split the launch velocity: u_y = 10 × sin 30° = 5 m/s and uₓ = 10 × cos 30° ≈ 8.66 m/s.
  2. Time of flight from the vertical motion: T = 2u_y / g = 2 × 5 / 10 = 1 s.
  3. Range from the horizontal motion using the same time: R = 8.66 × 1 ≈ 8.7 m.

Result: 1 s and about 8.7 m.

22

Rebuild it yourself

Explain why the range on level ground is the same for launch angles of 30° and 60° at the same speed.

Compare with a model answer

Range is u² sin 2θ / g. For 30° and 60°, 2θ is 60° and 120°, which have the same sine. The steeper launch stays up longer but moves sideways more slowly, and the two effects balance.

Practice

Source: Fixture Source Paper A (2020), Q8

A ball is thrown with speed 20 m/s at 30° above the horizontal. Find the time of flight. Take g = 10 m/s².

Hint

Find the vertical component of the launch velocity.

Worked answer
  1. u_y = 20 × sin 30° = 10 m/s.
  2. T = 2u_y / g = 2 × 10 / 10 = 2 s.

Answer: 2 s

A ball is launched at 20 m/s at 30° above the horizontal. Find the maximum height it reaches. Take g = 10 m/s².

Hint

At the top the vertical velocity is zero.

Worked answer
  1. u_y = 20 × sin 30° = 10 m/s.
  2. H = u_y² / 2g = 100 / 20 = 5 m.

Answer: 5 m

Find the horizontal range of a projectile launched at 20 m/s at 45° above level ground. Take g = 10 m/s².

Hint

Use R = u² sin 2θ / g.

Worked answer
  1. sin 2θ = sin 90° = 1.
  2. R = 20² × 1 / 10 = 40 m.

Answer: 40 m

Horizontal launch from a height

Key idea

For a horizontal launch the vertical motion starts from rest, so the fall time comes from h = ½gt², and the horizontal distance is the launch speed times that time.

When an object leaves a height h moving horizontally at u, its initial vertical velocity is zero. The fall time is therefore t = √(2h/g), exactly as for a dropped object.

24

Understand it

When an object leaves a height h moving horizontally at u, its initial vertical velocity is zero. The fall time is therefore t = √(2h/g), exactly as for a dropped object.

During that time the horizontal velocity stays u, so the horizontal distance is x = u × t = u√(2h/g).

At landing the velocity has components u horizontally and gt vertically, so the landing speed is √(u² + (gt)²).

Common slip: Using u in the vertical equation is a common slip; the launch speed is entirely horizontal.

25

Worked example

A ball rolls off a 5 m high wall at 4 m/s. Find where it lands and its landing speed. Take g = 10 m/s².

  1. Fall time from the vertical motion: 5 = ½ × 10 × t², so t = 1 s.
  2. Horizontal distance: x = 4 × 1 = 4 m from the foot of the wall.
  3. Vertical velocity at landing: v_y = gt = 10 m/s; landing speed = √(4² + 10²) ≈ 10.8 m/s.

Result: 4 m from the wall, at about 10.8 m/s.

26

Rebuild it yourself

Explain how you would find where a horizontally thrown object lands if you know only the launch speed and the height.

Compare with a model answer

Use the height with h = ½gt² to find the fall time, because the vertical motion starts from rest. Then multiply the unchanged horizontal speed by that time to get the horizontal distance.

Practice

A stone is thrown horizontally at 10 m/s from the top of a cliff 45 m high. Find the time to reach the ground and the horizontal distance travelled. Take g = 10 m/s².

Hint

The vertical motion starts from rest.

Worked answer
  1. 45 = ½ × 10 × t², so t² = 9 and t = 3 s.
  2. Horizontal distance = 10 × 3 = 30 m.

Answer: 3 s and 30 m

Source: Fixture Source Paper A (2020), Q7

A package is released from an aeroplane flying horizontally at 50 m/s at a height of 80 m. How far ahead of the release point does it land? Take g = 10 m/s².

Hint

The package keeps the aeroplane's horizontal velocity.

Worked answer
  1. Fall time: 80 = ½ × 10 × t², so t = 4 s.
  2. Horizontal distance = 50 × 4 = 200 m.

Answer: 200 m

Relative velocity in two dimensions

Key idea

The velocity of A relative to B is v_A − v_B. In two dimensions, subtract the vectors component by component or with a triangle.

An observer moving with velocity v_B sees every other object with its own velocity minus v_B. This is why rain that falls straight down appears slanted to a moving cyclist.

28

Understand it

An observer moving with velocity v_B sees every other object with its own velocity minus v_B. This is why rain that falls straight down appears slanted to a moving cyclist.

For a boat crossing a river, the ground velocity is the boat's velocity through the water plus the water's velocity; the two are perpendicular when the boat heads straight across.

Draw the vectors head to tail and use Pythagoras for the size and the tangent for the direction.

Common slip: Adding the speeds (4 + 3 = 7) ignores direction; perpendicular velocities combine by Pythagoras.

29

Worked example

A swimmer swims at 1.2 m/s straight across a river flowing at 0.5 m/s. Find the swimmer's speed relative to the bank.

  1. The swimmer's velocity through the water and the river's velocity are perpendicular.
  2. Combine them by Pythagoras: v = √(1.2² + 0.5²) = √(1.44 + 0.25) = 1.3 m/s.
  3. Direction: tan φ = 0.5 / 1.2, so the swimmer drifts about 23° downstream of straight across.

Result: 1.3 m/s, about 23° downstream.

30

Rebuild it yourself

Explain why rain falling straight down looks slanted to someone riding a bicycle.

Compare with a model answer

The cyclist sees the rain's velocity minus her own velocity. Subtracting her forward velocity adds a backward horizontal component to the rain's vertical velocity, so the rain appears to come towards her at a slant.

Practice

A boat moves at 4 m/s in still water. It heads straight across a river flowing at 3 m/s. Find the resultant speed of the boat relative to the bank.

Hint

The two velocities are perpendicular.

Worked answer
  1. v = √(4² + 3²).
  2. v = 5 m/s.

Answer: 5 m/s

Rain falls vertically at 6 m/s. A cyclist rides at 8 m/s. Find the speed of the rain relative to the cyclist.

Hint

Subtract the cyclist's velocity from the rain's velocity.

Worked answer
  1. v_rain − v_cyclist = (−8, −6) m/s.
  2. Relative speed = √(8² + 6²) = 10 m/s.

Answer: 10 m/s

Uniform circular motion

Key idea

Moving in a circle at constant speed still needs an acceleration of size v²/r, pointed towards the centre.

In uniform circular motion the speed is constant, but the direction of the velocity turns continuously, so the velocity vector changes and there is an acceleration.

32

Understand it

In uniform circular motion the speed is constant, but the direction of the velocity turns continuously, so the velocity vector changes and there is an acceleration.

That acceleration always points to the centre of the circle and has magnitude a = v²/r. It is called centripetal acceleration.

A faster speed or a tighter circle both need a larger centripetal acceleration.

Common slip: Constant speed does not mean zero acceleration when the path curves.

33

Worked example

A toy car moves round a circular track of radius 0.5 m at 1 m/s. Find its centripetal acceleration.

  1. Identify the speed and radius: v = 1 m/s and r = 0.5 m.
  2. Apply a = v²/r = 1² / 0.5 = 2 m/s².
  3. State the direction: towards the centre of the track at every instant.

Result: 2 m/s² towards the centre.

34

Rebuild it yourself

Explain in your own words why a body moving round a circle at constant speed is accelerating.

Compare with a model answer

Its velocity keeps changing direction, and acceleration measures any change in velocity, not just a change in speed. The change always points towards the centre.

Practice

A body moves in a circle of radius 2 m with a constant speed of 4 m/s. Find its centripetal acceleration.

Hint

Use a = v²/r.

Worked answer
  1. a = v²/r = 4² / 2.
  2. a = 8 m/s², directed towards the centre.

Answer: 8 m/s²
