Friction opposes relative sliding at a contact

Subject: PhysicsTopic: Laws of MotionConcept: Friction opposes relative sliding at a contactConcept difficulty: Medium concept
1

Before giving friction a direction, imagine the contact made perfectly slippery and ask which way the surfaces would slide relative to each other; friction opposes that, and is zero if there is no such tendency.

Medium concept
Section 1 of 2Next →

The key step

Friction is a contact force along the surface that opposes relative sliding, or the tendency for relative sliding, between the contacting surfaces; it is not defined by the object's overall velocity alone.

You need

  • Can choose one body and identify external contact interactions acting on it.
  • Assign each force arrow to the body on which that force acts.

This unit builds toward

  • INJSO|2023|28 Jan 2023|Physics|Q24 · D2
    The move learners miss: A tyre is part of the chosen system, so a force attributed to the tyre is not the external agent that slows the entire rider+cycle system; the ground is. Try it in Core2
  1. The step the hard question turns onChoose the body or system and name the exact contact before deciding friction; for a contact between two bodies, keep the two friction forces as an equal-and-opposite third-law pair on different bodies.
    Why valid: Friction belongs to one contact interaction. The force on A by B and the force on B by A are a third-law pair, so neither may be moved onto the other body's free-body diagram.
    Result: chosen body/system; named contact and partner; tangent direction; friction owner and receiver fixed.
  2. Imagine the relative sliding, or tendency to slide, at that named contact if tangential friction were absent; point friction on the chosen body opposite that relative tendency.
    Why valid: Contact friction resists relative sliding between the surfaces.
    Result: contact-specific slip tendency -> friction direction; direction need not oppose the body's velocity.
  3. Check a conveyor-belt case where the belt can frictionally accelerate an object in the same direction as the object's motion.
    Why valid: The counterexample falsifies the shortcut 'friction always points opposite velocity'.
    Result: friction direction follows contact slip tendency, not a universal page-direction rule.
  4. Decide whether any tangential friction is required at all; allow friction to be zero when the contact has neither relative sliding nor a tendency to slide.
    Why valid: Friction is a responsive tangential contact force, not an automatic nonzero force merely because two rough surfaces touch.
    Result: contact-state check -> friction may be zero when no tangential force is required.

Equations and validity

EquationWhat it tells youWhen you can use it
|fs|≤μsN

For a contact that remains static, the static-friction magnitude adjusts to the value required by the tangential dynamics up to the limiting value mu_s N; equality applies only at impending slip.

  • The surfaces are in contact and have no relative sliding during the state being tested.
  • N is obtained from the actual free-body dynamics, not assumed to equal mg.
  • Static friction opposes the impending relative slip direction.
  • Equality |f_s| = mu_s N is used only at impending slip.

Watch one

A book rests on a tray. A waiter first carries the tray at steady speed in a straight line, then speeds up forward; the book never slips. Find the friction force of the tray on the book in each stage, including whether it can be zero.

  1. Choose the book as the body; the contact is the tray's top surface, and friction acts along it.
  2. Steady carry: if the tray became perfectly slippery, the book would keep moving with the tray, so there is no slip tendency and the friction is zero.
  3. Speeding up: without friction the book would keep its old speed while the tray moved ahead, so the book would slip backward relative to the tray.
  4. Static friction opposes that tendency, so the tray's friction on the book points forward, the same way the book is moving.

Result

During the steady carry the friction is zero; while the tray speeds up, static friction on the book points forward.

Check

The book speeds up forward and friction is the only horizontal force on it, so by F_net = m a that force must point forward, which agrees.

Before giving friction a direction, imagine the contact made perfectly slippery and ask which way the surfaces would slide relative to each other; friction opposes that, and is zero if there is no such tendency.

A tempting wrong path

  • Friction always points opposite the object's velocity.
  • Two rough surfaces in contact must always exert a nonzero friction force.

Diagnose

  • A moving conveyor belt speeds up a box resting on it. Can the friction on the box point in the same direction as the box's motion?
  • A crate and rough platform already move together at constant velocity. If no horizontal force is otherwise required, must the friction force be nonzero merely because the surfaces are rough?

Repair

  • Decide the relative sliding tendency at the contact first; friction opposes that tendency.
  • No. Ask what relative sliding or slip tendency would occur without friction. If none exists and no tangential force is required, friction can be zero.

1-2-3 quick check

  1. 1 · CheckCHECK: For a chosen body, name the other surface at the contact and state which way that surface would slip relative to the body if friction vanished.
  2. 2 · ApplyAPPLY: A conveyor belt moves right faster than a box on it. Predict the friction on the box and the equal-and-opposite friction on the belt.
  3. 3 · ConnectCONNECT: Carry this contact ownership into the quantitative test: after direction is fixed, the next question is how much friction the no-slip motion requires and whether the contact can supply it.

Try it with less support

A box is placed on a conveyor belt moving to the right faster than the box. Before the box matches the belt speed, what direction is the friction force on the box?

Quantitative static and kinetic friction conditions

Subject: PhysicsTopic: Laws of MotionConcept: Quantitative static and kinetic friction conditionsConcept difficulty: Hard concept
1

Find the actual normal force and the friction that rest would need, compare that need with μ_s N, and use μ N as the friction value only at impending slip or once sliding.

Hard concept
Section 2 of 2← Previous

The key step

Static friction is a responsive contact force: solve for the friction required to prevent relative slip and compare it with the limiting value mu_s N. Equality is a threshold condition, while the kinetic model is used only after sliding is established.

You need

  • Can determine friction direction from relative slip tendency.
  • Can write Newton-II equations for a chosen body.
  • Identify friction as a contact force that opposes relative sliding or the tendency to slide.
  • Relate signed net external force to acceleration with F_net = m a.

  1. Build the chosen-body free-body diagram and solve the perpendicular dynamics for N before using any friction law: do not assume N = mg; N=mg only in the special level case with no other perpendicular force or acceleration; a downward added component raises N, an upward component lowers it, and if the required contact force would be negative the contact is lost so N=0 and friction=0.
    Why valid: A unilateral contact can push but not pull. Its normal reaction is whatever the perpendicular Newton-II equation requires while contact persists; friction capacity is then tied to that actual N.
    Result: perpendicular equation -> actual N≥0; level examples N=mg+F_down or N=mg-F_up; contact lost -> N=0 and f=0.
  2. Assume no relative sliding provisionally, choose a body or a whole connected system deliberately, and solve the tangential Newton-II equation for the friction demand at each rough contact.
    Why valid: Static friction responds to the no-slip dynamics. A system boundary can remove internal contact pairs, but it cannot create friction capacity at a smooth contact or move a rough contact from one body to another.
    Result: chosen boundary + no-slip dynamics -> signed f_required at each actual rough contact.
  3. Compare each |f_required| with μ_sN; on an incline this comparison can be turned into the angle condition for the onset of sliding rather than setting f=μ_sN from the start.
    Why valid: The static-contact model permits any required value only up to the limiting magnitude.
    Result: |f_required| <= mu_s N -> static feasible; equality only at impending slip; after sliding use the kinetic model. Equivalent symbolic form: |f_required|≤μ_sN.
  4. The step the hard question turns onAt the threshold use |f_s|=μ_sN; once relative sliding is established use |f_k|=μ_kN opposite the sliding, and if several contacts change state, update N and the friction model at each contact before continuing.
    Why valid: Static-below-limit, impending-slip, kinetic-sliding and contact-lost are different contact states. A multi-contact process may switch which contact is static or sliding, so stale μN values cannot be carried across a transition.
    Result: state map: static below limit -> f_required; impending -> μ_sN; sliding -> μ_kN; contact lost -> N=f=0.

|fk|=μkN

Once relative sliding at the contact is established, the bounded dry-sliding model uses kinetic-friction magnitude mu_k N, directed opposite the relative sliding.

  • Relative sliding between the two surfaces is established.
  • N is obtained from the actual free-body dynamics.
  • The friction direction opposes the relative sliding at the contact.
  • The simple dry-sliding coefficient model is being used.

Watch one

JEE (Advanced) · 2020 · Paper 1 · Q13 · Official past paper, faithful restatement of the original

A uniform metre scale is supported horizontally on two index fingers initially at 0.00 cm and 90.00 cm. Both fingers are moved slowly toward the centre. The slipping finger alternates because static and kinetic friction differ. With mu_s=0.40 and mu_k=0.32, find the final distance x_R of the right finger from the 50.00 cm centre at the instant it stops and the left finger starts slipping again.

  1. Treat the slowly moved metre scale as quasistatic and label the two finger supports by their current coordinates and normal reactions N_L and N_R.

    Why valid: At every instant the scale has negligible translational and angular acceleration, but the support reactions change as the finger positions change.

    Result: N_L+N_R=mg; their ratio is set by support positions.

  2. For each current pair of support positions, obtain N_L and N_R from force and torque balance before writing friction.

    Why valid: Friction capacities are μN, so a fixed normal reaction while a support moves would make the threshold condition inconsistent with the changing support geometry.

    Result: With the right finger at 90 cm, N_L/N_R=40/(50-l); the first switch gives l=18 cm.

  3. After the first switch, hold the left finger at 18 cm while the right finger slips inward and recompute the support ratio N_R/N_L=32/(r-50).

    Why valid: The support geometry has changed, so the normal reactions—and therefore both friction capacities—must be recalculated for the second stage rather than carried over.

    Result: N_R/N_L=32/(r-50).

  4. At the second stick-slip transition use kinetic friction at the slipping right contact and limiting static friction at the sticking left contact: μ_kN_R=μ_sN_L; this gives r-50=25.6 cm and swaps which finger slips.

    Why valid: The friction law is contact-state specific: a sliding contact uses μ_kN while a sticking contact can rise only to μ_sN. Equality marks the instant the roles switch.

    Result: x_R=25.6 cm.

Result

25.6 cm.

Check

The result is less than the initial 40 cm offset of the right finger and greater than zero, consistent with inward motion.

Find the actual normal force and the friction that rest would need, compare that need with μ_s N, and use μ N as the friction value only at impending slip or once sliding.

A tempting wrong path

  • Friction always equals mu N.
  • The normal force is always mg.
  • If both mu_s and mu_k are given, the coefficients themselves tell you which friction model to use.
  • Friction can keep acting after the surfaces lose contact, or a negative value of N can be used inside μN.

Diagnose

  • A small horizontal force is applied to a block that remains at rest. Must static friction already equal mu_s N?
  • If an extra force presses a block into a horizontal surface, can the normal reaction still be assumed equal to mg?
  • A problem gives both mu_s and mu_k but does not say whether the contact slips. Can you choose kinetic friction just because mu_k is supplied?
  • An upward pull becomes large enough that the floor would need a downward normal force to keep touching the block. What are N and friction after separation?

Repair

  • No. First solve the force required for no slip. Static friction matches that requirement up to its limit; equality occurs only at impending slip.
  • Determine N from the perpendicular free-body equation. N = mg is only a special case.
  • No. Determine the contact state first: test whether the no-slip friction demand fits within mu_s N. Use the kinetic model only after sliding is established.
  • A contact can push, not pull. Once the perpendicular equation would require N<0, contact is lost: set N=0 and therefore contact friction is zero.

1-2-3 quick check

  1. 1 · CheckCHECK: State the four contact states in order and the friction rule for each: static below its limit, impending slip, established sliding, and contact lost.
  2. 2 · ApplyAPPLY: A level block has weight 50 N and is pulled 20 N upward as well as 12 N sideways. With μ_s=0.5, find N, the static limit, and the actual friction if it stays at rest.
  3. 3 · ConnectCONNECT: In a two-contact problem, after one contact starts sliding, recompute the normal reactions and identify which contact is now static before writing the next μN equation.
