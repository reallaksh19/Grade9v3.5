The Mole Concept & Avogadro's Bridge

What is happening?

Atoms and molecules are too small to count individually. One mole of any substance contains exactly Avogadro's number ($N_A = 6.022 \times 10^{23}$) of elementary particles, with mass equal to its molar mass in grams.

Key Idea

The mole ($n$) is the universal translation hub: $\text{Mass} \leftrightarrow \text{Moles} \leftrightarrow \text{Particles} \leftrightarrow \text{Volume at STP}$.

$$n = \frac{m}{M} = \frac{N}{N_A} = \frac{V_{\text{STP}}}{22.4 \text{ L}}$$
  • Mass to Moles: Divide given mass $m$ in grams by the molar mass $M$ in $\text{g/mol}$.
  • Moles to Particles: Multiply moles $n$ by Avogadro's constant $N_A = 6.022 \times 10^{23} \text{ particles/mol}$.
  • Gas Standard
    Molar Volume at STP: At STP ($0^\circ\text{C}$ and $1\text{ atm}$), $1\text{ mole}$ of ANY ideal gas occupies exactly $22.4\text{ L}$ ($22400\text{ mL}$).

Chemical Formulae: The Criss-Cross Valency Rule

What is happening?

In an electrically neutral chemical compound, the total positive charge of the cations must exactly cancel the total negative charge of the anions.

Key Idea

Cross the numerical valencies as subscripts. Enclose polyatomic ions (e.g. $\text{SO}_4^{2-}, \text{NO}_3^-$) in parentheses whenever subscript $> 1$.

Common Ionic Formulations:

  1. Calcium Phosphate: $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ and $\text{PO}_4^{3-} \implies \mathbf{\text{Ca}_3(\text{PO}_4)_2}$.
  2. Ammonium Carbonate: $\text{NH}_4^+$ and $\text{CO}_3^{2-} \implies \mathbf{(\text{NH}_4)_2\text{CO}_3}$.
  3. Magnesium Nitride: $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ and $\text{N}^{3-} \implies \mathbf{\text{Mg}_3\text{N}_2}$.
  4. Aluminum Oxide: $\text{Al}^{3+}$ and $\text{O}^{2-} \implies \mathbf{\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3}$.

Molecular Mass, Formula Mass & Mass Percentage

The molecular mass is the sum of atomic masses of all atoms present in a molecular formula, expressed in unified atomic mass units ($\text{u}$) or $\text{g/mol}$.

1. Molecular Mass Calculation: Glucose ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6$)

  • $\text{C}: 6 \times 12.011 = 72.066\text{ u}$
  • $\text{H}: 12 \times 1.008 = 12.096\text{ u}$
  • $\text{O}: 6 \times 16.00 = 96.00\text{ u}$
  • Total Molar Mass: $180.16\text{ g/mol}$

2. Mass Percentage of Carbon in Glucose

$$\% \text{ by mass} = \frac{\text{Mass of element in 1 mol}}{\text{Molar mass of compound}} \times 100$$

$$\% \text{ C} = \frac{72.066}{180.16} \times 100 = \mathbf{40.0\%}$$

Satisfies the Law of Definite Proportions across all pure specimens.

Stoichiometry & Limiting Reagent Balance

What is happening?

Reactants are rarely combined in exact stoichiometric proportions. The reactant that is completely consumed first stops the reaction and limits the theoretical yield of products.

Key Idea

To identify the Limiting Reagent: divide available moles of each reactant by its balanced stoichiometric coefficient. The smallest quotient identifies the limiting reagent!

Rule of Smallest Quotient

For reaction: $a A + b B \to \text{Products}$

Compare $\frac{n_A}{a}$ vs $\frac{n_B}{b}$.

If $\frac{n_A}{a} < \frac{n_B}{b}$, then reactant $A$ is strictly limiting, and all product calculations MUST proceed from $n_A$.
