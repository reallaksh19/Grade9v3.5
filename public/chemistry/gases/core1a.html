Unit 1 of 6

Gas Particle Model: Spacing, Motion & Attraction

What is happening?

Particles of matter are in continuous random motion. In solids they vibrate in place; in liquids they slide past each other; in gases they move freely at high speeds with negligible intermolecular attraction.

Key Idea

Gas properties (fluidity, expansion, high compressibility) derive directly from massive intermolecular space and high particle kinetic energy.

  • Intermolecular Space: Gases possess huge intermolecular spaces ($> 100\times$ molecular diameter), enabling extreme compressibility.
  • Intermolecular Force: Attraction forces are minimum in gases and maximum in solids. Gas particles exert force only during collisions.
  • Crux Distinction
    Diffusion Rate: $\text{Rate of diffusion} \propto \text{Kinetic energy} \propto T$. Gases diffuse rapidly into other gases due to empty space and high molecular speeds.
⚠️ Watch Out:

Never say "gases flow because they have no mass". Gas particles have mass; they flow because intermolecular forces cannot hold them in fixed positions.

Unit 2 of 6

Compression & Liquefaction: Effect of Pressure & Temperature

What is happening?

Applying external pressure via a piston forces gas particles into closer proximity. Lowering temperature reduces molecular kinetic energy so attractive forces can bind them into a liquid.

Key Idea

Gases can be liquefied by: High Pressure + Low Temperature. Solid $\text{CO}_2$ (dry ice) sublimes directly back to gas when pressure drops to 1 atm.

Worked Scenario: Gas Under Piston Compression

Question: Why can massive volumes of natural gas or petroleum gas be stored in compact steel cylinders (CNG / LPG)?

  1. Gas particles have huge intermolecular space compared to solids and liquids.
  2. When large mechanical pressure is applied, this empty space is eliminated without particle deformation.
  3. Compressibility allows thousands of litres of gas to contract into a portable container under moderate pressures.

Unit 3 of 6

The Kelvin Scale & Thermal Energy Conversions

What is happening?

Temperature is a statistical measure of average particle kinetic energy. The Kelvin scale starts at absolute zero ($0\text{ K}$), where molecular motion ceases.

Key Idea

$T(\text{K}) = t(^\circ\text{C}) + 273.15$. The Kelvin scale has no negative values because thermal kinetic energy cannot be negative.

$$T \text{ (in Kelvin)} = t \text{ (in } ^\circ\text{C)} + 273.15$$

Quick Conversions:

  1. Convert $25^\circ\text{C}$ to Kelvin: $T = 25 + 273.15 = 298.15\text{ K}$.
  2. Convert $373\text{ K}$ to Celsius: $t = 373 - 273.15 = 99.85^\circ\text{C} \approx 100^\circ\text{C}$ (boiling point of water).
  3. Convert $0^\circ\text{C}$ to Kelvin: $T = 0 + 273.15 = 273.15\text{ K}$ (ice melting point).

Unit 4 of 6

Evaporation: Surface Phenomenon & Evaporative Cooling

What is happening?

In any liquid, particles at the surface have a distribution of speeds. Those with sufficient kinetic energy overcome intermolecular forces and escape into the vapor phase at any temperature below the boiling point.

Key Idea

Evaporation causes cooling. When high-energy particles leave, the remaining liquid's average kinetic energy decreases, lowering its temperature.

  • Surface Area $\uparrow$: More surface particles exposed $\implies$ higher rate of escape (e.g. saucer cools tea faster than cup).
  • Temperature $\uparrow$: Higher average kinetic energy $\implies$ greater fraction of particles cross escape threshold.
  • Wind Speed $\uparrow$: Rapid air movement sweeps water vapor molecules away, preventing local saturation.
  • Inverse Factor
    Humidity $\downarrow$: Humidity is the amount of water vapor already present in air. If air is already saturated, evaporation rate drops drastically.

Unit 5 of 6

Boiling vs. Evaporation & Latent Heat Plateaus

What is happening?

While heating water, temperature rises continuously until $100^\circ\text{C}$. Then temperature remains completely constant until all water converts into steam, despite heat being supplied continuously.

Key Idea

Latent Heat ($Q = m L$): The hidden thermal energy consumed solely to break intermolecular attraction bonds during state transition without raising kinetic energy.

Crucial Competitive Reasoning: Why Steam Burns Worse than Boiling Water

Question: Why does steam at $100^\circ\text{C}$ produce significantly more severe burns than boiling water at $100^\circ\text{C}$?

  1. Both are at the same temperature ($100^\circ\text{C}$), so their average particle kinetic energies are identical.
  2. However, particles in steam have absorbed an additional Latent Heat of Vaporization ($L_v \approx 2.26 \times 10^6\text{ J/kg}$) to overcome liquid intermolecular bonds.
  3. When steam contacts skin, it condenses first (releasing the enormous latent heat $L_v$), and then cools down as hot liquid, delivering double the heat payload to tissue.

Unit 6 of 6

Sublimation & Deposition: Direct Solid ⇄ Gas Transitions

What is happening?

Certain substances bypass the liquid state completely when heated at atmospheric pressure: Solid $\xrightarrow{\text{sublimation}} \text{Gas}$, and Gas $\xrightarrow{\text{deposition}} \text{Solid}$.

Key Idea

Common sublimable substances: Ammonium Chloride ($\text{NH}_4\text{Cl}$), Camphor, Naphthalene, Anthracene, and Solid Carbon Dioxide (Dry Ice).

Experimental Apparatus & Separation Technique

  1. Place a mixture of common salt ($\text{NaCl}$) and ammonium chloride ($\text{NH}_4\text{Cl}$) in a china dish.
  2. Invert a glass funnel over the dish and put a cotton plug on the funnel stem to prevent vapor loss.
  3. Heat gently with a burner. Ammonium chloride sublimes directly into white vapours.
  4. The vapours cool on the upper funnel walls and deposit back as pure crystalline solid, while non-volatile $\text{NaCl}$ remains in the dish.
