Force and laws of motion: Construction

Concept Book

Newton's first law and free-body diagrams

Learning promise

Grade-9 Force and Laws of Motion plus the owner-approved bounded Pinnacle preparation slice: balanced/unbalanced force reasoning, body-specific FBD ownership, friction direction, static-friction limits and kinetic-friction model, F_net = m a, Newton-third-law interaction pairs, common-acceleration connected bodies, ideal-string tension, and one fixed-pulley single-string length constraint. Observer-frame/pseudo-force reasoning remains an explicit-demand extension.

Concept route

  1. Zero net force allows steady motion
    1. Separate 'is moving' from 'is changing its velocity', and link zero net force only to the second.
  2. Nonzero forces can cancel
    1. List every force on the body as its own contribution, then combine them with signs; a zero sum never means that no forces act.
  3. A free-body diagram belongs to one chosen body
    1. Pick one body and admit only forces that answer 'who acts on this body?'; a partner force, or the components of a force already listed, never joins that list as an extra force.
  4. An accelerating observer needs a declared pseudo-force convention
    1. Say whether the observer is accelerating; keep the physical forces identical in both descriptions and add −m a_frame only for the accelerating observer.
  5. Net force, not any one force, sets acceleration
    1. Choose one body and a signed axis, add every external force on that body, and divide the net force by the mass; on a fixed incline, choose axes along and across the contact.
  6. Friction opposes relative sliding at a contact
    1. Before giving friction a direction, imagine the contact made perfectly slippery and ask which way the surfaces would slide relative to each other; friction opposes that, and is zero if there is no such tendency.
  7. Third-law partner forces act on different bodies
    1. Name each force of an interaction as 'A on B' or 'B on A', and put it on the diagram of the body it acts on, so the pair never meets in one sum.
  8. Quantitative static and kinetic friction conditions
    1. Find the actual normal force and the friction that rest would need, compare that need with μ_s N, and use μ N as the friction value only at impending slip or once sliding.
  9. Connected bodies and a justified common acceleration
    1. Name the physical link that fixes the separation, give both bodies one sign convention, and write a separate Newton-II equation for each body with the interaction force kept in it.
    2. Add the body equations into one system equation only where the internal pair should drop out, then isolate the body that makes the requested interaction force external.
  10. Ideal-string tension belongs to each body-specific FBD
    1. Declare the ideal string before writing a single T, then draw that tension on each attached body, pulling away from the body along its own segment.
    2. Check that nothing breaks the ideal model, then take the tension from a chosen body's signed Newton-II equation instead of setting it equal to a weight or an applied force.
  11. One fixed-pulley string gives one length constraint
    1. Measure both coordinates away from the fixed pulley, write the changing length as y_A + y_B = constant, and differentiate that one relation.
    2. Read the signed result back as motion, and keep the length argument for the accelerations separate from the ideal-pulley argument for equal tension.

Zero net force allows steady motion

Subject: PhysicsTopic: Laws of MotionConcept: Zero net force allows steady motionConcept difficulty: Medium concept
1

Separate 'is moving' from 'is changing its velocity', and link zero net force only to the second.

Medium concept
Section 1 of 14Next →

The key step

Zero net force does not mean zero motion; it means velocity does not change, so rest and steady motion are both compatible with the same condition.

You need

  • Can tell whether an object's velocity is changing.
  1. Separate the statement 'is moving' from the statement 'is changing its velocity.'
    Why valid: Motion and change of motion are different observations, and only the second identifies acceleration.
    Result: state A: moving steadily. state B: speeding up, slowing down, or turning.
  2. Imagine a low-friction cart after the hand has stopped touching it and compare successive equal-time snapshots.
    Why valid: Once the hand is gone, a continuing steady velocity can be observed without a continuing forward push.
    Result: successive equal-time snapshots -> equal forward position advances; velocity unchanged.
  3. Compare the released moving cart with a cart that starts at rest under the same zero-net-force condition.
    Why valid: If both keep their initial velocities, the rule is about unchanged velocity rather than about rest.
    Result: moving cart: stays steady. resting cart: stays at rest. both show no velocity change.

Equations and validity

EquationWhat it tells youWhen you can use it
Fnet=ma

The vector net external force on a chosen body equals its mass times its acceleration.

  • m > 0.
  • F_net and a refer to the same chosen body.
  • All signed components use one declared axis.

Watch one

An ice-hockey puck of mass 0.2 kg slides across smooth ice at 4 m/s after the stick loses contact. Friction and air resistance are negligible. What horizontal forces act on it, what is its velocity 3 s later, and would a puck at rest on the same ice start moving?

  1. Once the stick loses contact, nothing touches the puck horizontally, so the horizontal net force is zero.
  2. F_net = m a gives a = 0/0.2 = 0 m/s², so the velocity does not change: after 3 s it is still 4 m/s.
  3. Equal times give equal advances: 4 m in each second, 12 m in 3 s.
  4. A puck at rest under the same zero net force keeps its zero velocity, so it stays at rest.

Result

No horizontal force acts. The puck still moves at 4 m/s, having covered 12 m, and a puck at rest stays at rest.

Check

With even a small friction force added, the puck would slow down: the force changes the velocity rather than keeping it up.

Separate 'is moving' from 'is changing its velocity', and link zero net force only to the second.

A tempting wrong path

  • A force must keep acting in the direction of motion or the object must stop.

Diagnose

  • A low-friction cart is pushed and then released. After the hand loses contact, what horizontal force is required merely to keep it moving steadily?

Repair

  • Separate velocity from change of velocity: a net force is required for acceleration, not for the existence of motion.

1-2-3 quick check

  1. 1 · CheckPut back a small friction force: the object must now slow down, which shows that a force changes the velocity rather than maintaining it.
  2. 2 · Checka = F_net/m gives 0 N / m = 0 m/s², and distance equals speed × time in metres for equal time intervals.

Try it with less support

One cart is at rest and another moves steadily in a straight line. For each, decide whether zero net force is compatible with the motion and explain the common rule.

Nonzero forces can cancel

Subject: PhysicsTopic: Laws of MotionConcept: Nonzero forces can cancelConcept difficulty: Medium concept
1

List every force on the body as its own contribution, then combine them with signs; a zero sum never means that no forces act.

Medium concept
Section 2 of 14← PreviousNext →

The key step

Several nonzero forces can act on one body while their directed sum is zero; the motion responds to the net result rather than to the mere presence of forces.

You need

  • Can interpret zero net force as unchanged velocity.
  • Relate zero net force to unchanged velocity.
  1. List two opposite pushes on the same object as separate contributions before combining them.
    Why valid: Keeping the contributions visible prevents a zero total from being mistaken for no forces.
    Result: leftward push: present. rightward push: present.
  2. Make the two opposite pushes equal in size and combine their directional effects.
    Why valid: Equal opposite contributions cancel in the net result while each contribution still exists.
    Result: two nonzero opposite pushes -> net force: zero.
  3. Increase both opposite pushes together while keeping them equal.
    Why valid: The individual forces change while the zero net result persists, directly separating the parts from their sum.
    Result: larger opposite pushes: still net force zero; individual forces remain nonzero.

Equations and validity

EquationWhat it tells youWhen you can use it
Fnet=ma

The vector net external force on a chosen body equals its mass times its acceleration.

  • m > 0.
  • F_net and a refer to the same chosen body.
  • All signed components use one declared axis.

Watch one

A 1.5 kg lamp hangs at rest from a ceiling cord. Take g = 10 m/s² and up as positive. List each force on the lamp with its size, find the net force, and say what would happen if the cord were cut.

  1. Two agents act on the lamp: the Earth, through its weight, and the cord, through its tension.
  2. Weight: 1.5 × 10 = 15 N, so −15 N on the declared axis.
  3. The lamp is at rest, so F_net = 0: T − 15 = 0 and T = +15 N. Both forces are real and non-zero.
  4. Cut the cord and only the weight remains: F_net = −15 N and a = −15/1.5 = −10 m/s².

Result

The cord pulls up with 15 N and gravity pulls down with 15 N; the net force is zero. If the cord were cut, the lamp would accelerate downward at 10 m/s².

Check

The cut-cord case shows that the 15 N weight was acting all along; the tension had been cancelling it, not removing it.

List every force on the body as its own contribution, then combine them with signs; a zero sum never means that no forces act.

A tempting wrong path

  • If the net force is zero, no forces are acting.

Diagnose

  • A book rests on a table. Are there no forces on the book, or can nonzero forces add to zero?

Repair

  • List every interaction force on the body, then combine their directions; cancellation belongs to the net result, not to the individual forces.

1-2-3 quick check

  1. 1 · CheckRemove one of the balancing forces: the other must now produce an acceleration, which proves it was acting all along.
  2. 2 · CheckWith one axis declared, the balancing forces must carry opposite signs and equal sizes; two forces of the same sign can never sum to zero.

Try it with less support

An object has two equal opposite forces acting on it. Explain what is zero and what is not, then state what motion change the net result predicts.

A free-body diagram belongs to one chosen body

Subject: PhysicsTopic: Laws of MotionConcept: A free-body diagram belongs to one chosen bodyConcept difficulty: Hard concept
1

Pick one body and admit only forces that answer 'who acts on this body?'; a partner force, or the components of a force already listed, never joins that list as an extra force.

Hard concept
Section 3 of 14← PreviousNext →

The key step

A free-body diagram contains forces acting on one chosen body only; the equal-and-opposite partner of an interaction acts on the other body and belongs on that other body's diagram.

You need

  • Can keep several forces visible even when their net effect is zero.
  1. Choose the cart as the body to describe and require every arrow to answer 'who acts on the cart?'
    Why valid: The ownership question gives one mechanical test for whether a force belongs on the chosen body's diagram.
    Result: chosen body: cart. every listed arrow must be an agent -> cart force.
  2. For the horse-cart contact, place the horse-on-cart force on the cart diagram and place the cart-on-horse partner on a separate horse diagram.
    Why valid: The two forces belong to one interaction but act on different bodies, so combining them on one body's diagram changes the question being asked.
    Result: cart diagram: horse -> cart. horse diagram: cart -> horse.
  3. Switch the chosen body from cart to horse and rebuild the inventory from the ownership question.
    Why valid: A correct diagram changes when the chosen body changes even though the physical interaction is the same.
    Result: switch body -> switch which contact-force arrow belongs in the diagram; partner arrows never cancel on one diagram.
  4. When a physical force is resolved into components for chosen axes, keep the body-force inventory unchanged: use the components as a representation of that one force in the equations, not as additional interaction forces alongside the original vector.
    Why valid: Resolving a vector changes its coordinate representation, not the number of physical interactions acting on the body. Counting both the original force and its components double-counts the same interaction.
    Result: one physical force -> either original vector representation or its signed components in the chosen equations, never both as separate forces

Fnet=ma

The vector net external force on a chosen body equals its mass times its acceleration.

  • m > 0.
  • F_net and a refer to the same chosen body.
  • All signed components use one declared axis.

Watch one

A 50 kg child stands on a 20 kg box that rests on the floor. Take g = 10 m/s². Build the free-body inventory of the box: name each force, its agent, its direction and its size.

  1. Chosen body: the box. Every arrow must answer 'who acts on the box?'.
  2. Earth on box: weight 20 × 10 = 200 N down. Child on box: a contact push down; the child is at rest, so it equals the child's weight, 500 N.
  3. Floor on box: the box is at rest, so the floor pushes up with 200 + 500 = 700 N.
  4. The child's 500 N weight acts on the child, and the box's 500 N upward push acts on the child too; both belong on the child's diagram.

Result

On the box: its weight 200 N down (Earth), the child's push 500 N down (child), and the floor's push 700 N up (floor). The child's own weight is not on the box's diagram.

Check

Taking child and box together, the floor supports a total weight of 700 N, the same floor force the box's own diagram gives.

Pick one body and admit only forces that answer 'who acts on this body?'; a partner force, or the components of a force already listed, never joins that list as an extra force.

A tempting wrong path

  • Equal and opposite partner forces cancel each other on one body's free-body diagram.
  • After resolving weight on an incline, mg, mg sin(theta) and mg cos(theta) are three separate forces on the block.

Diagnose

  • A horse pulls a cart and the cart pulls back equally. Which of those two forces acts on the cart, and which acts on the horse?
  • If gravity is one Earth-on-block interaction, did choosing tilted axes create two new physical agents?

Repair

  • Choose one body first. Put only forces acting on that body on its diagram; draw the partner force only when the other body is chosen.
  • No. Weight remains one physical force. Its parallel/perpendicular components are the coordinate representation used in the chosen equations; do not add the original mg again to those component sums.

1-2-3 quick check

  1. 1 · CheckTreat the touching bodies as one system: the external supporting force it predicts must equal the one found from the single body's own diagram.
  2. 2 · CheckEach interaction contributes exactly one arrow to a given diagram; a diagram holding both members of a pair has one arrow that belongs to the other body.

Try it with less support

A person pushes a box. Describe the two contact-force partner arrows and state which one belongs on the box's free-body diagram.

An accelerating observer needs a declared pseudo-force convention

Subject: PhysicsTopic: Laws of MotionConcept: An accelerating observer needs a declared pseudo-force conventionConcept difficulty: Hard concept
1

Say whether the observer is accelerating; keep the physical forces identical in both descriptions and add −m a_frame only for the accelerating observer.

Hard concept
Section 4 of 14← PreviousNext →

The key step

A force-based account depends on the observer: a non-accelerating observer uses the physical interaction forces, while an accelerating observer must add the corresponding pseudo-force convention to describe the same observed motion consistently.

You need

  • Can build a force inventory for one chosen body.
  • Assign each force arrow to the body on which that force acts.
  1. Describe the same loose object first from the roadside and then from inside a bus, stating whether each observer is accelerating.
    Why valid: The observer choice is part of the model; leaving it implicit makes two different descriptions look contradictory.
    Result: roadside: observer acceleration zero. bus during start: observer acceleration forward.
  2. Keep the physical interactions unchanged and compare what the accelerating bus observer sees the loose object do relative to the bus.
    Why valid: The apparent backward motion comes from the observer's changing motion, not from a newly discovered physical contact on the object.
    Result: bus accelerates forward -> loose object appears to shift backward relative to the bus.
  3. Add a backward pseudo-force only in the accelerating-observer description and compare it with the roadside account.
    Why valid: The added convention compensates for the observer's acceleration without inventing a new physical interaction.
    Result: roadside account: physical forces only. accelerating-bus account: same physical forces plus backward pseudo-force.

Equations and validity

EquationWhat it tells youWhen you can use it
N−mg=may

For a person that remains in contact with a horizontal scale in an inertial ground frame, with upward positive and g the positive magnitude of gravitational acceleration, the signed vertical force balance is N - m g = m a_y.

  • The ground frame is treated as inertial and the positive vertical direction is upward.
  • The person remains in contact with the scale, so N >= 0.
  • The person and scale share the stated vertical acceleration while contact persists.
  • g is the positive magnitude of the downward gravitational acceleration.
Fpseudo=−maframe

In a one-axis frame accelerating with signed acceleration a_frame relative to an inertial frame, the pseudo-force assigned to a mass m is opposite the frame acceleration and has magnitude m|a_frame|.

  • The selected observer frame has nonzero translational acceleration relative to the inertial frame.
  • F_pseudo and a_frame use the same declared axis and sign convention.
  • The mass is positive and treated as constant.
  • The pseudo-force is a frame-dependent modelling term, not a physical interaction and not a Newton-third-law partner.
tan(θ)=|aframe|g

For a bob stationary relative to a horizontally accelerating vehicle, with a massless taut string and no other horizontal interaction, the equilibrium string tilt magnitude theta from vertical satisfies tan(theta)=|a_frame|/g in the vehicle frame.

  • The bob is stationary relative to the vehicle after transients, so its relative acceleration is zero.
  • The vehicle has constant horizontal acceleration over the represented state.
  • The string is massless and taut, and air resistance is neglected.
  • theta is measured from the vertical; the bob tilts opposite the vehicle acceleration.

Watch one

A 60 kg person stands on a scale in a lift that accelerates upward at 2 m/s². Take g = 10 m/s² and up as positive. Find the scale reading using the ground frame, then again using the lift frame with a pseudo-force, and compare.

  1. Ground frame: the physical forces are the scale's push N up and the weight 600 N down, and the person accelerates at +2 m/s².
  2. Ground-frame equation: N − 600 = 60 × 2, so N = 720 N.
  3. Lift frame: the person is at rest, and the frame accelerates at +2 m/s², so add F_pseudo = −m a_frame = −120 N.
  4. Lift-frame equation: N − 600 − 120 = 0, so N = 720 N, the same reading.

Result

Both descriptions give a scale reading of 720 N.

Check

With the lift at rest or moving steadily, a_frame = 0, the pseudo-force vanishes and both frames give N = 600 N.

Say whether the observer is accelerating; keep the physical forces identical in both descriptions and add −m a_frame only for the accelerating observer.

A tempting wrong path

  • The same ordinary-force diagram can be used unchanged in any accelerating observer's frame.

Diagnose

  • A loose mass appears to accelerate backward inside a bus that starts forward. Can the bus observer use only the same physical-force inventory as the roadside observer?

Repair

  • State the observer first. Use physical interactions alone for a non-accelerating observer; for an accelerating observer include the corresponding pseudo-force consistently.

1-2-3 quick check

  1. 1 · CheckBoth descriptions must predict the same observable, such as the scale reading or the string direction.
  2. 2 · CheckSet the frame's acceleration to zero: the pseudo-force must vanish and the two descriptions must become identical.

Try it with less support

A bus accelerates forward and a loose object appears to move backward relative to the bus. Compare the roadside and bus-observer force descriptions without calculating any magnitude.

Net force, not any one force, sets acceleration

Subject: PhysicsTopic: Laws of MotionConcept: Net force, not any one force, sets accelerationConcept difficulty: Medium concept
1

Choose one body and a signed axis, add every external force on that body, and divide the net force by the mass; on a fixed incline, choose axes along and across the contact.

Medium concept
Section 5 of 14← PreviousNext →

The key step

For a chosen body of positive mass in the Grade 9 inertial-frame model, acceleration points with the signed net external force and satisfies F_net = m a.

You need

  • Can choose one body, draw its external forces, and distinguish zero from nonzero net force.
  • Assign each force arrow to the body on which that force acts.
  1. Choose one body, declare a positive direction, and list only external forces acting on that body.
    Why valid: The net-force relation applies to one body's external force sum.
    Result: one body; one signed axis; signed external-force list.
  2. Add the signed forces to obtain F_net, then use F_net = m a.
    Why valid: The net external force on the chosen body sets its acceleration: F_net = m a holds for that one body, with m > 0, in an inertial frame.
    Result: F_net = signed sum; a = F_net / m.
  3. Return to an equal-opposite force case.
    Why valid: The relation must recover zero acceleration when the signed force sum is zero.
    Result: F_net = 0 -> a = 0 for m > 0.
  4. Choose axes that make the active constraint easy to express; for a fixed inclined contact, use parallel/perpendicular axes when helpful, resolve the physical forces into those axes, and write separate signed Newton-II equations.
    Why valid: Newton II is coordinate-independent. Aligning one axis with the contact makes maintained contact explicit as a_perp=0 while leaving the tangential acceleration free to be nonzero.
    Result: contact-aligned model: Sigma F_parallel = m a_parallel; Sigma F_perp = m a_perp, with maintained fixed contact -> a_perp = 0

Equations and validity

EquationWhat it tells youWhen you can use it
Fnet=ma

The vector net external force on a chosen body equals its mass times its acceleration.

  • m > 0.
  • F_net and a refer to the same chosen body.
  • All signed components use one declared axis.

Watch one

A 10 kg crate is pulled up a smooth ramp inclined at 30° by a rope parallel to the ramp with a force of 70 N. Take g = 10 m/s². Using axes along and perpendicular to the ramp, find the crate's acceleration and the normal force, and the pull that would give zero acceleration.

  1. Body: the crate. +x up the ramp, +y out of the ramp. Forces: the 70 N pull along +x, the 100 N weight vertically down, the normal force N along +y.
  2. Resolve the weight: 100 × sin 30° = 50 N down the ramp and 100 × cos 30° ≈ 86.6 N into the ramp.
  3. Along the ramp: F_net = 70 − 50 = 20 N, so a = 20/10 = 2 m/s² up the ramp.
  4. Across the ramp the crate keeps contact, so a_y = 0 and N ≈ 86.6 N. A 50 N pull would make F_net = 0 along the ramp, so a = 0.

Result

a = 2 m/s² up the ramp, N ≈ 86.6 N, and a 50 N pull would give zero acceleration.

Check

Units: 20 N / 10 kg = 2 m/s². On level ground (0°) the same method gives N = 100 N and no down-slope weight component.

Choose one body and a signed axis, add every external force on that body, and divide the net force by the mass; on a fixed incline, choose axes along and across the contact.

A tempting wrong path

  • Acceleration points with whichever individual force looks largest in the diagram.
  • If a block on a fixed incline has zero acceleration perpendicular to the plane, its total acceleration must be zero.

Diagnose

  • Two 10 N forces act opposite each other on a body. Does either force make the body accelerate in its direction?
  • Can a block remain in contact with a fixed plane while speeding up down the plane?

Repair

  • Add all signed external forces first; acceleration follows the net force, not an isolated arrow.
  • Yes. Maintained contact sets only a_perp=0. The parallel component a_parallel is determined independently by the net force along the plane.

1-2-3 quick check

  1. 1 · CheckThe net force in newtons divided by the mass in kilograms must give m/s².
  2. 2 · CheckFlatten the incline to 0°: the method must give N = mg and no down-slope weight component, the level-floor case.

Try it with less support

A 4 kg body has a net external force of +12 N along a declared axis. Find its acceleration.

Friction opposes relative sliding at a contact

Subject: PhysicsTopic: Laws of MotionConcept: Friction opposes relative sliding at a contactConcept difficulty: Medium concept
1

Before giving friction a direction, imagine the contact made perfectly slippery and ask which way the surfaces would slide relative to each other; friction opposes that, and is zero if there is no such tendency.

Medium concept
Section 6 of 14← PreviousNext →

The key step

Friction is a contact force along the surface that opposes relative sliding, or the tendency for relative sliding, between the contacting surfaces; it is not defined by the object's overall velocity alone.

You need

  • Can choose one body and identify external contact interactions acting on it.
  • Assign each force arrow to the body on which that force acts.
  1. Choose the body and mark the contact surface before deciding a friction direction.
    Why valid: Friction belongs to a specific contact interaction, so its direction must be read from relative motion at that contact.
    Result: chosen body; named contact; tangent direction available.
  2. Imagine the relative sliding or tendency to slide if friction were absent, then point friction opposite that relative tendency.
    Why valid: Contact friction resists relative sliding between the surfaces.
    Result: belt at 2 m/s, box at 0.5 m/s -> box slips left relative to the belt -> friction on the box points right
  3. Check a conveyor-belt case where the belt can frictionally accelerate an object in the same direction as the object's motion.
    Why valid: The counterexample falsifies the shortcut 'friction always points opposite velocity'.
    Result: friction direction follows contact slip tendency, not a universal page-direction rule.
  4. Decide whether any tangential friction is required at all; allow friction to be zero when the contact has neither relative sliding nor a tendency to slide.
    Why valid: Friction is a responsive tangential contact force, not an automatic nonzero force merely because two rough surfaces touch.
    Result: contact-state check -> friction may be zero when no tangential force is required.

Equations and validity

EquationWhat it tells youWhen you can use it
|fs|≤μsN

For a contact that remains static, the static-friction magnitude adjusts to the value required by the tangential dynamics up to the limiting value mu_s N; equality applies only at impending slip.

  • The surfaces are in contact and have no relative sliding during the state being tested.
  • N is obtained from the actual free-body dynamics, not assumed to equal mg.
  • Static friction opposes the impending relative slip direction.
  • Equality |f_s| = mu_s N is used only at impending slip.

Watch one

A book rests on a tray. A waiter first carries the tray at steady speed in a straight line, then speeds up forward; the book never slips. Find the friction force of the tray on the book in each stage, including whether it can be zero.

  1. Choose the book as the body; the contact is the tray's top surface, and friction acts along it.
  2. Steady carry: if the tray became perfectly slippery, the book would keep moving with the tray, so there is no slip tendency and the friction is zero.
  3. Speeding up: without friction the book would keep its old speed while the tray moved ahead, so the book would slip backward relative to the tray.
  4. Static friction opposes that tendency, so the tray's friction on the book points forward, the same way the book is moving.

Result

During the steady carry the friction is zero; while the tray speeds up, static friction on the book points forward.

Check

The book speeds up forward and friction is the only horizontal force on it, so by F_net = m a that force must point forward, which agrees.

Before giving friction a direction, imagine the contact made perfectly slippery and ask which way the surfaces would slide relative to each other; friction opposes that, and is zero if there is no such tendency.

A tempting wrong path

  • Friction always points opposite the object's velocity.
  • Two rough surfaces in contact must always exert a nonzero friction force.

Diagnose

  • A moving conveyor belt speeds up a box resting on it. Can the friction on the box point in the same direction as the box's motion?
  • A crate and rough platform already move together at constant velocity. If no horizontal force is otherwise required, must the friction force be nonzero merely because the surfaces are rough?

Repair

  • Decide the relative sliding tendency at the contact first; friction opposes that tendency.
  • No. Ask what relative sliding or slip tendency would occur without friction. If none exists and no tangential force is required, friction can be zero.

1-2-3 quick check

  1. 1 · CheckCompare with Newton's second law: if friction is the only horizontal force on the body, it must point the way the body's velocity is changing.
  2. 2 · CheckRemove every sideways push and every change of speed: the friction you predict must fall to zero.

Try it with less support

The key step

The key step

Static friction is a responsive contact force: solve for the friction required to prevent relative slip and compare it with the limiting value mu_s N. Equality is a threshold condition, while the kinetic model is used only after sliding is established.

You need

  • Can determine friction direction from relative slip tendency.
  • Can write Newton-II equations for a chosen body.
  • Identify friction as a contact force that opposes relative sliding or the tendency to slide.
  • Relate signed net external force to acceleration with F_net = m a.
  1. Build the chosen-body FBD and determine the normal reaction N from the force balance or acceleration perpendicular to the contact; do not assume N = mg.
    Why valid: The normal reaction is the contact force needed by the actual perpendicular dynamics and can differ from mg when the surface is inclined or other forces have perpendicular components.
    Result: perpendicular force equation -> actual N >= 0
  2. Assume no relative sliding provisionally and solve the tangential Newton-II equation for the static friction value f_required that would make that motion possible.
    Why valid: Static friction takes the value required by the no-slip state, provided the contact can supply it.
    Result: no-slip dynamics -> f_s = f_required
  3. Compare |f_required| with mu_s N.
    Why valid: The static-contact model permits any required value only up to the limiting magnitude.
    Result: |f_required| <= mu_s N -> static state feasible; |f_required| > mu_s N -> static state impossible
  4. Use |f_s| = mu_s N only at impending slip; if relative sliding is already established, use |f_k| = mu_k N with direction opposing the relative sliding.
    Why valid: The limiting equality and the sliding model describe different contact states and are not unconditional identities.
    Result: impending slip -> |f_s| = mu_s N; sliding -> |f_k| = mu_k N

Watch one

Masses A (3 kg) and B (1 kg) hang from one light inextensible string over a fixed, frictionless, light pulley, with y_A and y_B measured downward from the pulley. Take g = 10 m/s². Using a_A + a_B = 0 and one tension T, find a_A, a_B and T, and name the assumption each of those two relations rests on.

  1. The fixed total length gives a_A + a_B = 0 in these coordinates; this uses geometry only.
  2. One tension T is licensed separately, because the string is light and the pulley is light and frictionless.
  3. Down positive for each mass: A: 30 − T = 3a_A. B: 10 − T = 1 × a_B = −a_A.
  4. Subtracting B's equation from A's: 20 = 4a_A, so a_A = 5 m/s², a_B = −5 m/s², and T = 30 − 3 × 5 = 15 N.

Result

a_A = +5 m/s² (down), a_B = −5 m/s² (up) and T = 15 N. The acceleration relation rests on the fixed string length; the single T rests on the ideal pulley and string.

Check

As one system driven by the weight difference, (3 − 1) × 10 = 20 N on 4 kg gives 5 m/s², matching the two body equations.

A tempting wrong path

  • Bodies on opposite sides of a fixed pulley have the same signed acceleration.
  • The two end accelerations are equal and opposite because the string tension is equal on both sides.
