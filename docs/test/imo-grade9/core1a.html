Why three consecutive integers always multiply to a multiple of 6

A few successful calculations do not prove an assertion about every positive starting number. We will use an arbitrary n, guaranteed factors of 2 and 3, and a complete set of remainder cases to construct the proof.

Before startingComplete proofVisual tableWrong path and repairIndependent exit

Before starting

Check these assumptions:

  • Positive integer arithmetic and the meaning of a divisor.
  • Even and odd integers alternate when moving to the next integer.
  • A positive integer modulo 3 has residue 0, 1 or 2.
  • If two coprime integers divide a number then so does their product.

Conventions and scope

  • positive integer start: n is an arbitrary positive integer, not a chosen sample.
  • divisibility: a divides N means there exists an integer k with N=a*k.
  • modulo: Every integer has exactly one residue modulo m among 0,...,m-1.
  • factor ownership: A factor of 2 and a factor of 3 in the product may occur in different terms; 2 and 3 are coprime.

Core 1A · Complete construction

Decisive inference: Convert finite observations into a universal argument by establishing a guaranteed factor of 2 and a guaranteed factor of 3 within any three consecutive integers, and then use coprimality to conclude divisibility by 6.

Claim: For every positive integer n, the product n(n+1)(n+2) is divisible by 6.
  1. Step 1 · declare

    Set an arbitrary start

    Let n denote an arbitrary positive starting integer; the three consecutive factors are n, n+1 and n+2.

    Result: n(n+1)(n+2)

    Why this is valid: This representation includes every eligible triple, not merely three sampled values.

  2. Step 2 · transform

    Guarantee a factor of 2

    Consider n and n+1: one is even, so the product of the three integers has at least one factor of 2.

    Result: 2 divides n(n+1)(n+2)

    Why this is valid: Parity alternates in adjacent integers. The statement holds for both possible parities of n.

  3. Step 3 · transform

    Guarantee a factor of 3

    Separate n modulo 3 into cases 0, 1 and 2. Identify n, n+2 or n+1 respectively as divisible by 3.

    Result: 3 divides n(n+1)(n+2)

    Why this is valid: These three remainder classes are exhaustive and exactly one member of any three consecutive integers has remainder 0 modulo 3.

  4. Step 4 · verify

    Combine coprime factors

    Combine both divisors: since the whole product is divisible by 2 and 3 and these are coprime, it is divisible by 6.

    Result: 6 divides n(n+1)(n+2)

    Why this is valid: When coprime integers 2 and 3 divide the same whole number, their product 6 also divides that number; we have justified both divisibilities for every n.

What has been proved? No restriction was placed on which positive integer n might be; the proof uses only exhaustive residue and parity cases, so it applies to every allowed start.

Back from table to symbols: whichever of the three cases holds, 3 divides n(n+1)(n+2). In words, one of any three consecutive integers is a multiple of 3.

Parity remains a separate guarantee. If n is even then n supplies a factor of 2; if n is odd then n+1 supplies it. The factor 2 and factor 3 need not come from the same term. Their coprimality combines them in the whole product.

A plausible wrong path — and the repair

Misconception: Checking a few starting integers proves the divisibility assertion for all future n.

Diagnostic: If the starting integer is far larger than every sample you checked, what property guarantees divisibility without recalculation?

Repair: Use complete residue cases modulo 2 and 3 to cover all possible starts; identify guaranteed factors and apply the coprime-product rule.

Example values may be useful checks, but the universal guarantee comes from exhausting all modulo-2 and modulo-3 cases, not from trying selected values.

Task: Prove, for every positive integer n, that n(n+1)(n+2)(n+3) is divisible by 24. Give a general proof, not only examples.

Reveal model proof for self-check

The product of four consecutive positive integers is divisible by 24.

  1. Write the factors as n, n+1, n+2 and n+3, with n arbitrary and positive.
  2. Exactly one of these four has remainder 0 modulo 4, and another even factor has remainder 2 modulo 4. Their product is divisible by 8.
  3. Within any four consecutive integers there is a multiple of 3, so the full product is divisible by 3.
  4. Because 8 and 3 are coprime, the full product is divisible by 24 for every n.

Independent check: For each remainder n modulo 24, verify the four-factor product is a multiple of 24; this is an independent exhaustive modular check, not a replacement for the proof.
