Path travelled is not start-to-finish change

Subject: PhysicsTopic: MotionConcept: Path travelled is not start-to-finish changeConcept difficulty: Medium concept
1

Construction

Medium concept
Section 1 of 7Next →

The key step

Distance counts the whole route travelled, while displacement records only the directed change from start to finish; a return to the start leaves zero displacement after a nonzero journey.

You need

  • Can trace a route from a marked start to a marked finish on a straight line.
  1. Mark a start point on a straight path and declare east as the positive direction.
    Why valid: A directed change is only readable once the line has a stated positive way.
    Result: start: S. east: positive. west: opposite.
  2. Trace a walk away from S and then back to S, counting every part of the route.
    Why valid: The route count accumulates what was travelled even though the final place can match the initial place.
    Result: route travelled: nonzero. start-to-finish change: zero because finish = S.
  3. Compare that return trip with a one-way trip chosen to have the same total route length.
    Why valid: Holding route length fixed while changing the finish isolates the information displacement carries.
    Result: same route length; different finish -> different displacement.

Construction 1

A tempting wrong path

  • Distance travelled and displacement are always the same number.

Diagnose

  • A walker leaves a marker, turns around, and finishes back at the marker. Is the journey length zero just because the final change in position is zero?

Repair

  • Count every part of the route for distance; compare only the start and finish for displacement, keeping the declared direction.

Try it with less support

Route A leaves a marker and returns to it. Route B travels the same total amount but ends away from the marker. Without calculating a value, compare their distance and displacement.

Zero velocity at an instant does not mean zero acceleration

Subject: PhysicsTopic: MotionConcept: Zero velocity at an instant does not mean zero accelerationConcept difficulty: Medium concept
1

Construction

Medium concept
Section 2 of 7← PreviousNext →

The key step

Velocity and acceleration answer different questions: velocity can be zero at one instant while a nonzero acceleration determines the change through that instant.

You need

  • Can distinguish the direction of motion just before and just after a turning point.
  • Distinguish travelled path length from the directed start-to-finish change.
  1. Record three moments around a vertical turning point: just before the top, at the top, and just after.
    Why valid: A single snapshot hides change; neighbouring moments reveal whether the zero-velocity state persists.
    Result: before: moving upward. top: velocity zero. after: moving downward.
  2. Compare the direction of the velocity change across those three moments.
    Why valid: Going from upward through zero to downward requires the velocity to keep changing through the top instant.
    Result: velocity change through the top: downward, including at the zero-velocity instant.
  3. Contrast the turning point with an object that is simply resting on a shelf.
    Why valid: Both can have zero velocity now, but only one immediately changes velocity, so zero velocity alone cannot determine acceleration.
    Result: turning point: zero velocity with continuing change. shelf: zero velocity with no motion change in the comparison.
  4. Use v=0 only as the event condition for the vertical turning point while keeping the signed gravitational acceleration nonzero in the same instant.
    Why valid: Velocity describes the instantaneous state of motion; acceleration describes how that velocity is changing. At the apex the upward velocity has just decreased to zero and immediately continues toward negative values under the same downward acceleration.
    Result: apex event: v=0 at one instant; acceleration remains downward, so +up gives a=-g and +down gives a=+g

Construction 1

A tempting wrong path

  • If velocity is zero at an instant, acceleration must also be zero at that instant.

Diagnose

  • At the highest point of a vertically thrown object, velocity is momentarily zero. Does the next moment have the same velocity?

Repair

  • Compare moments immediately before and after the top; the velocity changes from upward to downward, so acceleration remains nonzero through the zero-velocity instant.

Try it with less support

Two objects have zero velocity at one instant: one is at the top of a vertical flight, the other is resting on a shelf. What can you conclude about their accelerations from velocity alone?

Average speed and average velocity come from different totals

Subject: PhysicsTopic: MotionConcept: Average speed and average velocity come from different totalsConcept difficulty: Medium concept
1

Construction

Medium concept
Section 3 of 7← PreviousNext →

The key step

Average speed divides total path length by total time, while average velocity divides signed displacement by that same time; neither is obtained by averaging listed speeds unless the weighting justifies it.

You need

  • Can distinguish total travelled path from signed displacement and can read one positive elapsed interval.
  • Distinguish travelled path length from the directed start-to-finish change.
  1. For one two-leg trip, record total path length, signed start-to-finish displacement, and the total elapsed time in separate rows.
    Why valid: The two averages differ only in the numerator, so separating the totals prevents path and endpoint information from being merged.
    Result: distance_total = sum of leg lengths; Delta x = x_final - x_initial; Delta t = total elapsed time.
  2. Divide total path length by total time, then divide signed displacement by the same total time.
    Why valid: Each relation is a total-over-common-interval average and the gate owns both formulas.
    Result: speed_avg = distance_total / Delta t; v_avg = Delta x / Delta t.
  3. Check a return trip where the finish equals the start.
    Why valid: The boundary keeps nonzero path length while forcing displacement to zero, so it tests whether the two averages stayed distinct.
    Result: return trip -> average speed may be nonzero; whole-trip average velocity = 0.

Equations and validity

EquationWhat it tells youWhen you can use it

speed_avg = distance_total / Delta t

Average speed is total distance travelled divided by the positive elapsed time.

  • Delta t > 0.
  • distance_total >= 0.

v_avg = Delta x / Delta t

Average velocity is signed displacement divided by the same positive elapsed time.

  • Delta t > 0.
  • Delta x uses one declared axis/sign convention.

Construction 1

A tempting wrong path

  • Average speed is always the arithmetic mean of the speeds listed for the trip.

Diagnose

  • Two equal-distance legs are travelled at different speeds. Did the traveller spend equal time at each speed?

Repair

  • Build total distance and total time first. Only equal time weighting permits an unweighted arithmetic mean of speeds.

Try it with less support

A trip covers 120 m of total path, finishes 40 m in the positive direction from its start, and lasts 20 s. Find the average speed and average velocity.

Derive the constant-acceleration equations from one velocity-time graph

Subject: PhysicsTopic: MotionConcept: Derive the constant-acceleration equations from one velocity-time graphConcept difficulty: Medium concept

The key step

One straight velocity-time segment carries all three Grade 9 kinematic equations: its slope gives v = u + at, its rectangle-plus-triangle area gives s = ut + 0.5at^2, and combining trapezoid area with the slope relation eliminates time to give v^2 = u^2 + 2as.

You need

  • Can read a velocity-time graph, calculate a segment slope, and calculate rectangle, triangle and trapezoid areas.
  • Can distinguish a constant-slope velocity-time segment from a curved changing-acceleration segment.
  • Plot and interpret position-time and velocity-time graphs, using interval slope to obtain average velocity or average acceleration.
Section 4 of 7← PreviousNext →
  1. Read the straight velocity-time graph from (0,u) to (t,v) and identify one constant slope.
    Why valid: A straight velocity-time segment means one acceleration value describes the whole interval.
    Result: slope = (v-u)/t = a; graph area = signed displacement s.
  2. Rearrange the straight-line slope equation.
    Why valid: The graph slope is the constant acceleration for the interval.
    Result: a = (v-u)/t -> a t = v-u -> v = u + a t.
  3. Split the graph area into the initial-velocity rectangle and the acceleration triangle, then substitute v-u = a t.
    Why valid: REL-CONSTANT-ACCELERATION-GRAPH-AREA owns the displacement area and the two regions exactly partition it.
    Result: s = u t + 0.5 (v-u)t -> s = u t + 0.5 (a t)t = u t + 0.5 a t^2.
  4. Write the same graph area as a trapezoid, substitute t = (v-u)/a from the slope, and factor the difference of squares.
    Why valid: Both equations describe the same straight graph interval, so the common time variable can be eliminated.
    Result: s = 0.5(u+v)t; t=(v-u)/a -> 2as=(u+v)(v-u)=v^2-u^2 -> v^2=u^2+2as.
  5. Replace the straight segment with a curved velocity-time trace and ask whether the same graphical derivation is exact over the whole interval.
    Why valid: A curved trace has changing slope, so one constant a no longer describes the interval and the straight-line rectangle/triangle construction is not the exact graph.
    Result: curved v-t trace -> changing a -> the constant-a graphical derivation does not apply exactly to the whole interval.
  6. For bounded vertical motion in a region where local gravitational acceleration can be treated as constant, choose one positive vertical direction and translate the downward gravitational acceleration into one signed constant a before using any kinematic equation; do not change the sign of a when the velocity reverses.
    Why valid: The sign of acceleration is set by the declared coordinate axis and the physical downward direction of gravity, not by whether the object is currently moving upward or downward.
    Result: +up -> a=-g; +down -> a=+g, where g>0 is the local acceleration magnitude; v may change sign while a keeps the same signed value over the constant-g interval

Equations and validity

EquationWhat it tells youWhen you can use it

a_avg = Delta v / Delta t

Average acceleration over an interval is signed velocity change divided by the positive elapsed time.

  • Delta t > 0.
  • Velocity values use one declared one-dimensional sign convention.

s = u t + 0.5 (v-u) t = 0.5 (u+v) t

For constant acceleration, signed displacement over the interval is the signed geometric area under the straight velocity-time segment.

  • Acceleration is constant over the whole interval, so the velocity-time segment is straight.
  • u, v and s use one declared one-dimensional sign convention.
  • t >= 0.

v = u + a t

For constant acceleration over the interval, final velocity equals initial velocity plus acceleration times elapsed time.

  • Acceleration is constant over the whole interval.
  • u, v and a use one declared one-dimensional sign convention.
  • t >= 0.

s = u t + 0.5 a t^2

For constant acceleration, signed displacement equals the initial-velocity contribution plus the acceleration contribution.

  • Acceleration is constant over the whole interval.
  • s, u and a use one declared one-dimensional sign convention.
  • t >= 0.

v^2 = u^2 + 2 a s

For constant acceleration, squared velocities and signed displacement satisfy the no-time kinematic relation.

  • Acceleration is constant over the whole interval.
  • u, v, a and s use one declared one-dimensional sign convention.

Derive the constant-acceleration equations from one velocity-time graph

A tempting wrong path

  • The constant-acceleration equations are independent formulas to memorize rather than consequences of one straight velocity-time graph.
  • Gravitational acceleration is -g while the object rises and +g after it starts falling.

Diagnose

  • If the velocity-time graph is curved, can the same rectangle-plus-triangle derivation still use one constant a for the whole interval?
  • If +up is fixed for the whole trip, did the physical direction of gravity reverse when the object's velocity changed from upward to downward?

Repair

  • Return to the graph: slope must be constant. Derive the equations from that slope and the graph area before using them.
  • No. Keep the chosen axis fixed. With +up, gravity remains a=-g throughout the bounded flight; only velocity changes sign at the turning point.

Try it with less support

A velocity-time graph is a straight segment from 5 m/s at t = 0 to 11 m/s at t = 3 s. Use the graph slope to identify a, use its rectangle-plus-triangle area to find displacement, and show how the same graph yields v = u + at, s = ut + 0.5at^2, and v^2 = u^2 + 2as.

Uniform circular motion keeps speed while velocity turns

Subject: PhysicsTopic: MotionConcept: Uniform circular motion keeps speed while velocity turnsConcept difficulty: Medium concept
1

Construction

Medium concept
Section 5 of 7← PreviousNext →

The key step

An object moving around a circle at constant speed is still accelerating because velocity direction changes continuously; velocity is tangent, acceleration points inward, and the constant speed for one revolution is circumference divided by period: v = 2 pi r / T.

You need

  • Can distinguish speed from directed velocity and can recognize acceleration as a change of velocity.
  1. Mark equal-length velocity arrows tangent to a circle at several positions.
    Why valid: Equal arrow length represents constant speed while tangent direction follows the instantaneous motion.
    Result: same speed; different tangent velocity directions.
  2. Compare successive velocity arrows and identify the change in velocity direction.
    Why valid: Velocity changes whenever either its magnitude or direction changes.
    Result: At two different points A and B: |v_A| = |v_B| = v, but v_A and v_B point in different tangent directions, so Delta v = v_B - v_A is nonzero.
  3. Use symmetry at opposite points to check that the required acceleration is directed toward the centre rather than along the tangent.
    Why valid: A tangential acceleration would change speed, while inward turning changes direction while preserving speed in the ideal uniform case.
    Result: uniform circular motion -> acceleration inward; velocity tangent.
  4. For one complete uniform revolution, divide the circumference 2 pi r by the positive revolution period T.
    Why valid: REL-UNIFORM-CIRCULAR-SPEED is the gate-owned Grade 9 speed expression.
    Result: v = 2 pi r / T; for r = 7 m and T = 14 s, v = pi m/s ≈ 3.14 m/s.

Equations and validity

EquationWhat it tells youWhen you can use it

v = 2 pi r / T

For one complete uniform circular revolution, speed equals circumference divided by the positive period.

  • r > 0.
  • T > 0.
  • Motion is uniform and the path is circular over the measured revolution.

Construction 1

A tempting wrong path

  • Constant speed means zero acceleration.

Diagnose

  • A car moves around a circular track with a steady speedometer reading. Is its velocity unchanged?

Repair

  • Compare the tangent directions at different points: the direction changes, so velocity changes and acceleration is nonzero.

Try it with less support

A runner moves uniformly around a circular track of radius 7 m and completes one revolution in 14 s. Find the speed, then state the qualitative directions of velocity and acceleration at any point.

Motion graphs turn coordinate change into rate

Subject: PhysicsTopic: MotionConcept: Motion graphs turn coordinate change into rateConcept difficulty: Medium concept
1

Construction

Medium concept
Section 6 of 7← PreviousNext →

The key step

On a position-time graph, segment slope is average velocity over that interval; on a velocity-time graph, segment slope is average acceleration. Graph height and graph slope answer different physical questions.

You need

  • Can distinguish position, velocity and acceleration and can compute an interval average rate from two values.
  • Compute average speed from total distance and average velocity from signed displacement over one common interval.
  1. Read the axis names, units and two plotted coordinates before describing the motion.
    Why valid: A graph only acquires physical meaning through its labelled quantities and coordinate values.
    Result: position-time graph: horizontal t in s, vertical x in m; velocity-time graph: horizontal t in s, vertical v in m/s.
  2. On the position-time graph, subtract two position coordinates and divide by their time separation.
    Why valid: REL-AVERAGE-VELOCITY is the gate-owned interval relation and the graph supplies Delta x and Delta t.
    Result: between (2 s, 4 m) and (4 s, 8 m): v_avg = (8 - 4)/(4 - 2) = +2 m/s.
  3. On the velocity-time graph, subtract two velocity coordinates and divide by their time separation.
    Why valid: REL-AVERAGE-ACCELERATION is the gate-owned interval relation and the graph supplies Delta v and Delta t.
    Result: between (2 s, 3 m/s) and (4 s, 5 m/s): a_avg = (5 - 3)/(4 - 2) = +1 m/s^2.
  4. Use horizontal segments as boundary checks.
    Why valid: A horizontal position-time segment has zero position change, while a horizontal velocity-time segment has zero velocity change.
    Result: horizontal x-t -> v_avg = 0; horizontal v-t -> a_avg = 0.

Equations and validity

EquationWhat it tells youWhen you can use it

v_avg = Delta x / Delta t

Average velocity is signed displacement divided by the same positive elapsed time.

  • Delta t > 0.
  • Delta x uses one declared axis/sign convention.

a_avg = Delta v / Delta t

Average acceleration over an interval is signed velocity change divided by the positive elapsed time.

  • Delta t > 0.
  • Velocity values use one declared one-dimensional sign convention.

Construction 1

A tempting wrong path

  • A higher point on a motion graph always means the object is moving faster.

Diagnose

  • Two position-time graph points are high on the page but connected by a horizontal segment. Is the object moving quickly during that interval?

Repair

  • Read slope, not page height, for rate: a horizontal position-time segment has zero average velocity.

Try it with less support

On a velocity-time graph, velocity changes from +3 m/s at 2 s to +5 m/s at 4 s. Find the average acceleration over that interval and state what a horizontal velocity-time segment would mean.

Circular dynamics: centripetal is a net-force role, not a new force

Subject: PhysicsTopic: MotionConcept: Circular dynamics: centripetal is a net-force role, not a new forceConcept difficulty: Hard concept

The key step

The inward acceleration requirement v^2/r fixes only the net radial component m v^2/r; tension, friction, gravity, normal force, or combinations may supply that resultant, so 'centripetal force' must not be added as another interaction.

You need

  • Can identify tangent velocity and inward acceleration in elementary uniform circular motion.
  • Can draw a chosen-body FBD and apply Newton II along a declared axis.
  • Explain why uniform circular motion can have constant speed while velocity changes continuously, and calculate that speed from radius and revolution period.
  • Net force, not any one force, sets acceleration (Physics)
Section 7 of 7← Previous
  1. Hold speed constant and compare tangent velocity arrows at nearby points before showing any force.
    Why valid: Direction change establishes nonzero inward acceleration without invoking a force label.
    Result: velocity tangent; acceleration inward.
  2. Quantify the inward acceleration as a_r=v^2/r.
    Why valid: REL-CIRC-RADIAL-ACCELERATION is the gate-owned curvature relation.
    Result: a_r = v^2/r; for v=4 m/s and r=2 m, a_r=8 m/s^2.
  3. Choose one body and draw only real interactions, then declare inward-positive radial/tangential axes.
    Why valid: Force ownership and sign choice must precede Newton-II components.
    Result: real-force FBD with radial components.
  4. Set the signed real-force radial resultant equal to m v^2/r.
    Why valid: REL-CIRC-NET-RADIAL-FORCE is Newton II applied to the established radial acceleration.
    Result: Sigma F_r=m v^2/r without an extra Fc arrow.
  5. Switch among string, flat-curve friction, and gravity-supplied mechanisms while holding the same radial requirement.
    Why valid: The force type can change while the centripetal role remains the net radial resultant.
    Result: mechanism changes; radial invariant remains.
  6. Remove the string interaction and predict the immediate inertial path.
    Why valid: Without inward tension, instantaneous velocity remains tangent at the cut event.
    Result: initial post-cut path tangent, not radially outward.
  7. At vertical-circle top and bottom, re-declare inward and write the corresponding signed radial equations.
    Why valid: Gravity keeps its physical downward direction while the radial positive direction changes around the circle.
    Result: top T+mg=mv_top^2/r; bottom T-mg=mv_bottom^2/r.
  8. At the top impose the just-taut boundary T=0 to derive the minimum top speed.
    Why valid: A string cannot push, so negative tension signals failure of the taut-string model.
    Result: v_min=sqrt(gr).

Equations and validity

EquationWhat it tells youWhen you can use it

a_r = v^2 / r

For uniform circular motion of speed v on a path of positive radius r, the acceleration magnitude toward the centre is v^2/r.

  • r > 0.
  • The path is circular at the represented instant.
  • For the acceleration to be exactly radial with no tangential component, speed is constant at that instant/over the uniform model.

Sigma F_r = m v^2 / r

With inward chosen as positive radial direction, the signed sum of real radial force components equals m v^2/r for uniform circular motion.

  • Choose one body and declare inward as positive radial direction before resolving forces.
  • Only real interactions appear in an inertial-frame free-body diagram.
  • The represented motion has radial acceleration v^2/r.
  • Do not add an extra force named centripetal; Sigma F_r is the resultant radial role of the real forces.

T + m g = m v_top^2 / r

At the top of a vertical circle for a mass on a taut string, inward is downward so tension and weight both contribute positively to the radial resultant.

  • The body is at the top of the vertical circle.
  • The string is taut and massless in the bounded model.
  • Gravity acts downward and the radial positive direction is inward (downward at the top).

T - m g = m v_bottom^2 / r

At the bottom of a vertical circle for a mass on a taut string, inward is upward so tension contributes positively and weight negatively to the radial resultant.

  • The body is at the bottom of the vertical circle.
  • The string is taut and massless in the bounded model.
  • Gravity acts downward and the radial positive direction is inward (upward at the bottom).

v_min = sqrt(g r)

At the just-taut top boundary T=0, the vertical-circle top radial equation gives v_min=sqrt(gr).

  • The body is at the top of the vertical circle.
  • The string is at the just-taut boundary, so T = 0.
  • r > 0 and g > 0.
  • This is a boundary result for the declared string model, not a generic circular-motion formula.

Circular dynamics: centripetal is a net-force role, not a new force

A tempting wrong path

  • Tension plus an additional centripetal force both point inward.
  • A cut string makes the object fly radially outward.
  • Static friction on a flat curve always equals mu_s N.

Diagnose

  • A mass moves in a horizontal circle on one string. Which horizontal force arrows belong on its inertial-frame FBD?
  • At the instant a circular-motion string is cut, which direction does the object initially move?
  • At a low speed on a high-friction flat curve, must friction already be at its maximum?

Repair

  • Draw the string tension only; 'centripetal' names the role of the net inward component.
  • Keep the instantaneous tangent velocity; removing tension removes inward acceleration, not the tangent velocity.
  • Compute m v^2/r first and compare that demand with the static bound.

Try it with less support

A 2 kg mass moves at 4 m/s in a horizontal circle of radius 2 m on a string. Draw the inertial-frame horizontal FBD and find the required inward net force. Then state the immediate path if the string is cut.
