MOLE-MOD-01

Laws of Chemical Combination & Dalton's Theory

Part 1 · Core Concepts
🎯 Learning Target: Apply the Law of Conservation of Mass, Definite Proportions, and Multiple Proportions to reaction stoichiometry.
🧠 Memory Anchor
Definite Proportions: Same compound always contains elements in fixed mass ratio. Multiple Proportions: If two elements form >1 compound, masses of B combining with fixed mass of A are in simple whole-number ratios.
⚙️ Why It Works
Atoms are indivisible units of chemical reaction (Dalton). Compounds form by whole-atom combinations, requiring simple whole-number stoichiometric mass ratios.
🔍 Worked Contrast
Carbon oxides: CO has 12g C with 16g O. CO₂ has 12g C with 32g O. Mass ratio of O combining with 12g C is 16:32 = 1:2 (simple whole number!).
✍️ Active Practice & Step-by-Step Working

Problem: Two oxides of nitrogen contain 63.6% and 46.7% nitrogen respectively. Show that these results illustrate the Law of Multiple Proportions.

Reveal Checked Model Answer
In Oxide 1: 63.6g N combines with 36.4g O => 1g N combines with 36.4 / 63.6 = 0.572g O. In Oxide 2: 46.7g N combines with 53.3g O => 1g N combines with 53.3 / 46.7 = 1.141g O. Ratio of oxygen masses combining with 1g of nitrogen = 0.572 : 1.141 = 1 : 2.0 (exact simple whole number ratio 1:2). Hence Law of Multiple Proportions is proved (N₂O and NO).
⚠️ Common Trap to Avoid
Confusing Law of Definite Proportions with Multiple Proportions. Definite applies to ONE compound; Multiple compares TWO DIFFERENT compounds formed by the same elements.

MOLE-MOD-02

The Mole Concept & Avogadro's Number

Part 2 · Core Concepts
🎯 Learning Target: Convert inter-convertibly between mass, moles, number of entities, and molar volume at STP.
🧠 Memory Anchor
The Universal Mole Bridge: n = mass / Molar_mass = N / N_A = Volume_STP / 22.4 L (where N_A = 6.022 × 10²³).
⚙️ Why It Works
One mole contains exactly as many elementary entities as there are carbon atoms in 0.012 kg of ¹²C (Avogadro constant). It scales microscopic atomic mass units (u) directly to macroscopic grams (g).
🔍 Worked Contrast
1 mole of O atoms = 16.0 g = 6.022 × 10²³ atoms. 1 mole of O₂ molecules = 32.0 g = 6.022 × 10²³ molecules = 2 × 6.022 × 10²³ atoms = 22.4 L at STP.

✍️ Active Practice & Step-by-Step Working

Problem: Calculate the total number of electrons present in 1.4 g of dinitrogen gas (N₂).

Reveal Checked Model Answer
Molar mass of N₂ = 28 g/mol. Moles of N₂ = 1.4 g / 28 g/mol = 0.05 mol. Number of N₂ molecules = 0.05 × 6.022 × 10²³ = 3.011 × 10²² molecules. Each N₂ molecule has 7 + 7 = 14 electrons. Total electrons = 14 × 3.011 × 10²² = 4.215 × 10²³ electrons.
⚠️ Common Trap to Avoid
Failing to distinguish between atoms and molecules. Asking for 'atoms in 32g O₂' gives 2 × N_A, not 1 × N_A!

MOLE-MOD-03

Empirical and Molecular Formula Derivation

Part 3 · Core Concepts
🎯 Learning Target: Determine simplest integer mole ratios from mass percentage and deduce molecular formula from molar mass.
🧠 Memory Anchor
Empirical = Simplest whole-number ratio of atoms. Molecular = n × (Empirical Formula), where n = Molar Mass / Empirical Formula Mass.
⚙️ Why It Works
Combustion analysis and mass spectrometry provide elemental percentages. Dividing each mass percent by atomic mass yields relative mole counts; dividing by the smallest value normalizes to whole-number empirical subscripts.
🔍 Worked Contrast
Glucose: Molecular formula is C₆H₁₂O₆ (mass 180). Empirical formula is CH₂O (mass 30). Ratio n = 180 / 30 = 6.
✍️ Active Practice & Step-by-Step Working

Problem: An organic compound contains 40.0% C, 6.7% H, and 53.3% O by mass. Its molar mass is 60 g/mol. Find its empirical and molecular formulas.

Reveal Checked Model Answer
Moles of C = 40.0 / 12 = 3.33. Moles of H = 6.7 / 1 = 6.70. Moles of O = 53.3 / 16 = 3.33. Simplest ratio: C = 3.33/3.33 = 1, H = 6.70/3.33 = 2, O = 3.33/3.33 = 1. Empirical Formula = CH₂O (Empirical mass = 12 + 2 + 16 = 30). n = Molar Mass / Empirical Mass = 60 / 30 = 2. Molecular Formula = (CH₂O)₂ = C₂H₄O₂ (Acetic acid).
⚠️ Common Trap to Avoid
If mole ratios yield a fractional value like 1.33 or 1.5, do NOT round to nearest integer! Multiply all subscripts by 3 (for .33) or by 2 (for .5) to clear fractions.

MOLE-MOD-04

Stoichiometry & Limiting Reagent Analysis

Part 4 · Core Concepts
🎯 Learning Target: Identify the limiting reactant by mole-to-coefficient ratio and calculate theoretical yield.
🧠 Memory Anchor
Limiting Reagent Test: For each reactant, compute (Available Moles) / (Stoichiometric Coefficient). The SMALLEST value identifies the Limiting Reagent.
⚙️ Why It Works
Reactions proceed on an atom-by-atom basis dictated by balanced equation coefficients. The reactant that runs out first halts the reaction, dictating the maximum yield of products.
🔍 Worked Contrast
50 kg N₂ + 10 kg H₂ → NH₃. Moles N₂ = 50,000 / 28 = 1785.7 mol (Ratio = 1785.7/1 = 1785.7). Moles H₂ = 10,000 / 2.016 = 4960.3 mol (Ratio = 4960.3/3 = 1653.4). H₂ ratio is smaller => H₂ is Limiting Reagent!
✍️ Active Practice & Step-by-Step Working

Problem: 3.0 g of H₂ reacts with 29.0 g of O₂ to yield H₂O. (a) Which is the limiting reagent? (b) Calculate the maximum mass of H₂O formed.

Reveal Checked Model Answer
Reaction: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O. Moles of H₂ = 3.0 / 2.016 = 1.488 mol. Ratio = 1.488 / 2 = 0.744. Moles of O₂ = 29.0 / 32.0 = 0.906 mol. Ratio = 0.906 / 1 = 0.906. Since 0.744 < 0.906, H₂ is the LIMITING REAGENT. 2 moles H₂ produce 2 moles H₂O => 1.488 moles H₂ produce 1.488 moles H₂O. Mass of H₂O formed = 1.488 mol × 18.015 g/mol = 26.8 g H₂O.
⚠️ Common Trap to Avoid
Never pick the limiting reagent based solely on smaller mass. Always divide moles by stoichiometric coefficients!

MOLE-MOD-05

Concentration of Solutions: Molarity, Molality & Mole Fraction

Part 5 · Core Concepts
🎯 Learning Target: Calculate solution concentrations and convert between Molarity (M), Molality (m), and Mole Fraction (χ).
🧠 Memory Anchor
Molarity M = moles solute / Litres solution (Temperature DEPENDENT). Molality m = moles solute / kg solvent (Temperature INDEPENDENT).
⚙️ Why It Works
Solution volume expands with temperature changes, altering Molarity. Mass does not expand with temperature; hence Molality and Mole Fraction remain strictly invariant with temperature.
🔍 Worked Contrast
Dilution equation: M₁V₁ = M₂V₂ (conserves total moles of solute upon solvent addition).

✍️ Active Practice & Step-by-Step Working

Problem: Calculate the molarity of NaOH in the solution prepared by dissolving 4 g in enough water to form 250 mL of the solution.

Reveal Checked Model Answer
Molar mass of NaOH = 23 + 16 + 1 = 40 g/mol. Moles of NaOH = 4 g / 40 g/mol = 0.1 mol. Volume of solution in litres = 250 mL / 1000 = 0.250 L. Molarity M = moles / Litres = 0.1 mol / 0.250 L = 0.4 mol/L (0.4 M).
⚠️ Common Trap to Avoid
In molality, the denominator is MASS OF SOLVENT in kg, NOT total mass of solution!

Q1 · NCERT Unit 3 Q1

Ratio of Combining Masses in Water

Core 2 NCERT Helper

In water (H₂O), what is the ratio of the mass of hydrogen to the mass of oxygen, and how much oxygen is required to react completely with 3 g of hydrogen gas?

💡 See the Idea (Visual First Move)
Chemical formula H₂O has 2 H atoms (2 × 1 = 2 u) and 1 O atom (16 u). Fixed mass ratio = 2:16 = 1:8.
👨‍🏫 Teacher-Style Helper
Use Law of Definite Proportions: 1 g H requires 8 g O.
✍️ Your Working & Answer Commit
Reveal Answer & Step-by-Step Solution
Immediate Answer: Mass ratio H : O = 1 : 8. For 3 g of hydrogen, 3 × 8 = 24 g of oxygen is required.

Complete Solution:
1. Molar mass contribution: 2 H = 2 g, 1 O = 16 g. 2. Ratio of mass = 2 : 16 = 1 : 8. 3. By Law of Definite Proportions, 1 g hydrogen requires 8 g oxygen. 4. Therefore 3 g hydrogen requires 3 × 8 = 24 g oxygen.

Q2 · NCERT Unit 3 Q3

Formula Unit Mass of Sodium Carbonate

Core 2 NCERT Helper

Calculate the formula unit mass of: (a) Na₂CO₃·10H₂O (Washing Soda), (b) Ca(OH)₂ (Slaked Lime).

💡 See the Idea (Visual First Move)
Sum atomic masses of all atoms indicated in the formula unit.
👨‍🏫 Teacher-Style Helper
Atomic masses: Na = 23, C = 12, O = 16, H = 1, Ca = 40. Remember 10H₂O = 10 × 18 = 180 u.
✍️ Your Working & Answer Commit
Reveal Answer & Step-by-Step Solution
Immediate Answer: (a) Na₂CO₃·10H₂O = 286 u, (b) Ca(OH)₂ = 74 u.

Complete Solution:
1. Na₂CO₃·10H₂O = (2 × 23) + 12 + (3 × 16) + 10 × (2 × 1 + 16) = 46 + 12 + 48 + 180 = 286 u. 2. Ca(OH)₂ = 40 + 2 × (16 + 1) = 40 + 34 = 74 u.

Q3 · NCERT Unit 3 Q10

Number of Molecules in Sulphur

Core 2 NCERT Helper

Calculate the number of molecules of sulphur (S₈) present in 16 g of solid sulphur.

💡 See the Idea (Visual First Move)
First find molar mass of S₈ = 8 × 32 = 256 g/mol. Then moles, then multiply by N_A.
👨‍🏫 Teacher-Style Helper
N = (mass / Molar mass) × 6.022 × 10²³.
✍️ Your Working & Answer Commit
Reveal Answer & Step-by-Step Solution
Immediate Answer: 3.76 × 10²² molecules of S₈.

Complete Solution:
1. Molar mass of S₈ = 8 × 32 = 256 g/mol. 2. Moles of S₈ = 16 g / 256 g/mol = 0.0625 mol. 3. Number of molecules = 0.0625 × 6.022 × 10²³ = 3.764 × 10²² molecules.

Q4 · NCERT Unit 1 Q5

Temperature Conversion Discipline

Core 2 NCERT Helper

Convert the following temperatures to the Celsius and Kelvin scales: (a) 300 K to °C, (b) 573 K to °C, (c) 25°C to K, (d) 373°C to K.

💡 See the Idea (Visual First Move)
Relationship: T(K) = T(°C) + 273.15.
👨‍🏫 Teacher-Style Helper
Celsius = Kelvin - 273; Kelvin = Celsius + 273.
✍️ Your Working & Answer Commit
Reveal Answer & Step-by-Step Solution
Immediate Answer: (a) 27°C, (b) 300°C, (c) 298 K, (d) 646 K.

Complete Solution:
1. 300 K - 273 = 27°C. 2. 573 K - 273 = 300°C. 3. 25°C + 273 = 298 K. 4. 373°C + 273 = 646 K.
